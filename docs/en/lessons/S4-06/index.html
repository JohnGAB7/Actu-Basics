The birthday problem

In a group of 23 people, what is the probability that two share a birthday? Intuition says "very small", but the right answer is over 50%. The trick: you do not count the 22 pairs that involve you, but the 253 pairs of the whole group. And you go through the complement, "all different". Worked exercise at the end. Fictional data. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

Learning objectives

  • Count the pairs in a group with n(n-1)/2
  • Compute the probability that all birthdays differ, then use the complement
  • Explain why intuition fails

Prerequisites

  • S4.04: combinations
  • S4.05: complement and union

23 people in a room: do two share a birthday?

Twenty-three people in a room. What is the probability that two share the same birthday, day and month? Many answer 'a few percent'. The right answer, in the classic model, is over fifty percent.

Why intuition fails

First, understand where intuition goes wrong.

What you count, what the group counts

Each person instinctively thinks of the twenty-two pairs they form with the others. But the question is not about you: it is about a coincidence anywhere in the group. And the group has two hundred and fifty-three pairs, not twenty-two.

  • You: your 22 pairs with the others
  • The group: all possible pairs
  • C(23, 2) = 253 pairs

Number of pairs = n(n − 1) / 2

This is the formula for the number of pairs in a group of n: n times n minus one, divided by two. For twenty-three: twenty-three times twenty-two over two, two hundred and fifty-three.

\[nb_pairs(n) = n × (n - 1) / 2\]

The method: go through the complement

Counting coincidences directly would be painful. Go through the opposite: all different.

P(all different) = 365/365 × 364/365 × … × 343/365

The first person picks a birthday among three hundred and sixty-five days: three hundred and sixty-five over three hundred and sixty-five. The second must fall on a different day: three hundred and sixty-four over three hundred and sixty-five. The third, on one of the remaining three hundred and sixty-three. And so on, up to the twenty-third. Multiply all these ratios.

\[P(all diff) = prod((365 - k) / 365 for k in range(n))\]

The calculation for 23 people

Apply it.

Result for n = 23

The product is about zero point four hundred and ninety-three. Its complement — the probability of at least one match — is one minus that: zero point five hundred and seven. Fifty point seven percent. From twenty-three people, it is already more likely than not.

  • P(all different) ≈ 0.493
  • P(at least one match) = 1 − 0.493
  • ≈ 0.507, or 50.7%

What happens as n grows?

And with more people?

The probability rises very fast

At thirty people, seventy percent. At forty, almost nine in ten. At fifty, ninety-seven percent. As n grows, the number of pairs grows as n squared: eventually you have so many that a coincidence becomes almost certain.

  • n = 23: 50.7%
  • n = 30: 70.6%
  • n = 40: 89.1%
  • n = 41: 90.3%
  • n = 50: 97.0%

Two mistakes to avoid

Two traps.

The two classic mistakes

First mistake: twenty-three divided by three hundred and sixty-five. That is the probability one specific person shares your birthday, not the probability of a coincidence anywhere in the group. Second mistake: assuming the two hundred and fifty-three pairs are independent. They are not: if A and B match, and A and C match, then B and C match too.

  • Thinking you should compute 23 / 365: that is the probability one specific person shares your birthday
  • Thinking the pairs are independent: they are not

Your turn

Your turn.

Exercise

From how many people does the probability of a match exceed 90%?

From how many people does the probability of at least one shared birthday exceed ninety percent? Pause.

Solution

At forty people, eighty-nine percent: not yet. At forty-one, ninety point three percent: there we are. The answer is forty-one people.

  • Find the smallest n such that 1 − P(all different) ≥ 0.9
  • n = 40: 89.1% (not yet)
  • n = 41: 90.3% — there we are
  • Answer: 41 people
Answer : 41 people

Key points

Key points. In a group of n people, there are n times n minus one over two pairs, not n pairs. For the probability of at least one shared birthday, go through 'all different' and take the complement. And intuition fails because it only counts the pairs involving you.

  • Pairs in a group of n: n(n − 1) / 2
  • Go through the complement, all different
  • Intuition does not count all pairs
