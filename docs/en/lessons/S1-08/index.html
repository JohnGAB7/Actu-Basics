1% or 50%? Percentages and points

A rate goes from 2% to 3%: did it rise by 1% or by 50%? Both answers can be defended, as long as you say "percent of what". Learn to tell percentage points from percentage change, see why +50% then −50% doesn't bring you back where you started, and compare two towns with a rate per 100,000. Made-up examples, all numbers checked. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

A rate goes from 2% to 3%. Up 1%, or up 50%?

A rate goes from two percent to three percent. Did it rise by one percent, or by fifty percent?

CHALLENGE

From 2% to 3%: 1% or 50%?

Pick your answer before we go on. One percent, or fifty percent?

Percent of what?

To answer, we first need to go back to what a percentage is.

A percentage is a share of a base

A percentage is a part divided by a base, times one hundred. In a class of thirty students, six cycle to school. Six out of thirty, times one hundred: twenty percent.

\[\mathrm{percentage}=\dfrac{\mathrm{part}}{\mathrm{base}}\times 100\]

A percentage without its base means nothing. Always ask: percent of what?

A percentage without its base means nothing. The right reflex is always to ask: percent of what?

Points or percent?

Back to our rate, going from two to three percent.

From 2% to 3%: two ways to measure

There are two ways to measure this change, and both are correct. The first measures the gap between the two rates: three minus two, one percentage point. The second measures the change relative to where we started: a gap of one, divided by the starting rate of two, which is a fifty percent increase.

  • The gap: 3 − 2 = 1 percentage point
  • The change: (3 − 2) ÷ 2 = +50%

change, for a gap of 1 point

+50%

So here's the answer to the challenge: both numbers describe the same change. A fifty percent rise, for a gap of one percentage point.

Percentage change

Percentage change is always worked out the same way: the new value minus the old value, divided by the old value, times one hundred. This time, the base is the starting value.

\[\mathrm{change}=\dfrac{\mathrm{new}-\mathrm{old}}{\mathrm{old}}\times 100\]

Saying "+1%" for +1 point: a common point of confusion

So the only wrong answer is to say the rate went up by one percent. A one percent rise would take the rate from two percent to two point zero two percent. When you compare two percentages, talk about the gap in points, or the change in percent, and say which one you mean.

Percentages don't add up

Second trap: stacking two percentages.

+50%, then −50%

Start with a price of one hundred euros. It goes up by fifty percent: multiply by one point five, and it's now one hundred fifty euros. Then it drops by fifty percent: multiply by zero point five, and it falls to seventy-five euros.

  • Start: €100
  • +50%: 100 × 1.5 = €150
  • −50%: 150 × 0.5 = €75

You multiply the factors

To stack changes, you don't add them. You multiply their factors. One point five times zero point five is zero point seven five. Overall, that's a twenty-five percent drop. The fall applies to a price that has already gone up, so to a bigger base.

\[1.5\times 0.5=0.75\]

Exercise

A price rises by 20%, then falls by 20%. What's the overall effect?

Your turn. A price rises by twenty percent, then falls by twenty percent. What's the overall effect?

Solution

A twenty percent rise means multiplying by one point two. A twenty percent fall means multiplying by zero point eight. One point two times zero point eight: zero point nine six. The final price is four percent lower than where it started.

  • Factors: 1.2 and 0.8
  • 1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96
Answer : The final price is 4% lower

Count, proportion, rate

One last tool: comparing groups of different sizes.

Two towns, one disease

Take a fictional example: two towns and the same disease, counted over the same observation period and with the same case definition. Town A has fifty cases among twenty thousand residents. Town B has one hundred twenty cases among one hundred thousand residents.

  • Town A: 50 cases among 20,000 people
  • Town B: 120 cases among 100,000 people

Cases counted over the same period, with the same definition.

A rate per 100,000 puts everything on the same scale

To compare them, work out a rate per one hundred thousand people: the number of cases, divided by the population, times one hundred thousand. Town A gets two hundred fifty cases per hundred thousand. Town B gets one hundred twenty. Town B has more cases, but the observed rate is about twice as high in town A.

  • A: 50 ÷ 20,000 × 100,000 = 250
  • B: 120 ÷ 100,000 × 100,000 = 120
  • B has more cases, A has a higher rate

A count tells you how many. A proportion or a rate lets you compare.

Remember the difference. A count tells you how many cases there are. A proportion or a rate divides those cases by a base, and that's what lets you compare. In a real-world comparison, we would also need to check whether the populations are comparable, including their age structures.

Key takeaways

Let's recap. One: a percentage always has a base, so ask what it is. Two: the gap between two rates is in points, their change is in percent. Three: to stack changes, multiply the factors. Four: to compare groups of different sizes, use a rate, not a count.

  • Always ask: percent of what?
  • Gaps in points, changes in percent
  • Stacking changes: multiply the factors
  • Comparing groups: use a rate, not a count
