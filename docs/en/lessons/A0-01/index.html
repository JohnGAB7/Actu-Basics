Can maths put a price on risk?

Will your phone be stolen this year? Nobody knows. Yet an insurer can tell you today what covering it should cost. The person behind that number is an actuary.

This series: actuarial science from A to Z

Welcome to the first episode of our actuarial science series. Step by step, we will build the tools actuaries use, from the value of money over time to the solvency of an insurer. In this episode: what actuaries do, what an insurance contract is, and your very first actuarial calculation.

What actuaries do

Let's start with the job itself.

One job: putting numbers on uncertain futures

An actuary uses probability, statistics and finance to measure risks that lie in the future. How many accidents will happen next year? How long will a pensioner live? How much money must be set aside today to pay those future costs? Actuaries do not predict the future for a single person: they work with groups, with averages and with the uncertainty around them.

Where actuaries work

Actuaries work in several fields. In life insurance and pensions, they deal with how long people live, and with savings and retirement income. In non-life insurance, they price and reserve for cars, homes and businesses. In health insurance, they model medical costs. And in banks, consulting firms and risk teams, they measure and manage financial risks.

  • Life insurance and pensions
  • Non-life insurance: car, home, business
  • Health insurance
  • Banks, consulting, risk management

Their questions: how likely, how much, and when?

Across all these fields, the same three questions come back. How likely is the event? How much will it cost if it happens? And when will the money have to be paid? The whole series is built around these three questions.

What is an insurance contract?

Before any formula, we need to know what is being priced.

Meet JG's Holding, our insurer

Throughout this series, we will follow an insurer called JG's Holding. It is a fictional company, and all of its figures are made up. Its first product is simple: insurance against phone theft.

Inside a phone theft contract

Every insurance contract answers the same questions. The cover says which event is insured: here, the theft of the phone. The exclusions list what is not covered: in our fictional contract, for example, a lost phone, accidental damage, or a theft that was never reported. They depend on the terms of each contract. The premium is the amount the policyholder pays. The benefit is what the insurer pays if the event happens. The deductible is the part of the loss the policyholder keeps. And the term is how long the contract lasts: here, one year, then it can be renewed.

  • Cover: theft of the phone
  • Exclusions (examples): loss, damage, theft left unreported
  • Premium: what the policyholder pays
  • Benefit: what the insurer pays after a theft
  • Deductible: the part the policyholder keeps
  • Term: one year, then renewal

Examples from our fictional contract. Cover and exclusions depend on each contract's terms.

The price is set before the cost is known

Here is what makes insurance unusual. A baker knows the cost of flour before setting the price of bread. An insurer must set its price at the start of the year, before it knows how many phones will be stolen. That is exactly why it needs actuaries: to estimate a cost that is still in the future.

Your first actuarial calculation

Let's put a first number on this risk.

JG's Holding insures 1,000 phones for a year

JG's Holding insures one thousand phones for one year. Suppose each phone has a two percent chance of being stolen during the year. A stolen phone costs five hundred dollars to replace. On average, we then expect one thousand times two percent, that is twenty thefts. Twenty thefts at five hundred dollars each is an expected cost of ten thousand dollars. Shared among one thousand policyholders, that is ten dollars per phone.

  • Probability of theft: 2% per phone per year
  • Cost of a stolen phone: $500
  • Expected thefts: 1,000 × 2% = 20
  • Expected cost: 20 × $500 = $10,000
  • Per phone: $10,000 ÷ 1,000 = $10

Fictional figures. Simplified model: at most one theft per phone per year.

The pure premium

This ten dollars is called the pure premium. In this simplified example, it is the probability of a theft, p, times what the insurer pays if it happens, C: zero point zero two times five hundred, which gives ten dollars. In general, the pure premium is the expected claim cost per policy, with nothing added.

\[\mathrm{pure\ premium}=p\times C\]

Why the price is higher than $10

But JG's Holding cannot sell this cover for ten dollars. It has expenses: staff, computer systems and the cost of handling each claim. It may pay commissions to the shops or brokers that sell the contract. It needs a margin for uncertainty, because twenty thefts is only an average: one year there may be twelve, another year thirty. And it must hold capital, so that a bad year does not ruin it.

  • Expenses: staff, systems, claims handling
  • Commissions paid to sellers
  • A margin for uncertainty: 20 is only an average
  • Capital to survive a bad year

Pure premium + loadings = commercial premium

The price the customer actually pays is called the commercial premium. It starts from the pure premium and may include loadings for expenses, commissions, risk and the cost of capital. Whether the insurer makes a profit or a loss depends on all of these pieces, not only on the probability of theft. We will come back to this in detail in module A2.

How to become an actuary

So how do you become one?

Routes to qualification depend on the country

The route to becoming a qualified actuary depends on where you work. In the United States and Canada, the Society of Actuaries and the Casualty Actuarial Society run series of professional exams. The Institute and Faculty of Actuaries, based in the United Kingdom, runs its own exams, taken in many countries. In some European countries, an accredited university programme is one route into the profession. Requirements vary by country, so always check the relevant national actuarial association. Everywhere, the foundations are the same: mathematics, statistics and finance, followed by years of practical experience.

  • United States and Canada: SOA and CAS exams
  • United Kingdom and beyond: IFoA exams
  • Some European countries: an accredited degree is one route
  • Everywhere: maths, statistics, finance, then experience

Requirements change over time: always check the official website of each actuarial body.

The skills you will build in this series

Here are the skills this series will help you build. Probability and statistics, to describe uncertain events. Financial mathematics, to compare money paid at different dates. Spreadsheets first, then R or Python, to do the calculations at scale. And one skill that is often forgotten: explaining your numbers clearly to the people who have to make decisions.

  • Probability and statistics
  • Financial mathematics
  • Spreadsheets, then R or Python
  • Explaining numbers to people who decide

Exercise

JG's Holding adds a $100 deductible. What is the new pure premium per phone?

Your turn. JG's Holding adds a one hundred dollar deductible: after a theft, the policyholder pays the first hundred dollars. Same thousand phones, same two percent chance of theft. What is the new pure premium per phone?

Solution

Let's work it out. After a theft, JG's Holding now pays five hundred minus one hundred, that is four hundred dollars. The expected number of thefts does not change: still twenty. The expected cost becomes twenty times four hundred, that is eight thousand dollars. Shared among one thousand phones, the pure premium drops to eight dollars per phone.

  • After a theft, JG's Holding pays $500 − $100 = $400
  • Expected thefts: still 20
  • Expected cost: 20 × $400 = $8,000
Answer : Pure premium: $8,000 ÷ 1,000 = $8 per phone

Key takeaways

Key takeaways. Actuaries put numbers on future costs that are still uncertain. An insurance contract defines the cover, the exclusions, the premium, the benefit, the deductible and the term. The pure premium is the probability of the event times its cost. And the price the customer pays adds loadings on top of it.

  • Actuaries put numbers on uncertain future costs
  • A contract defines cover, exclusions, premium, benefit, deductible, term
  • Pure premium = probability × cost
  • The price paid adds loadings on top
