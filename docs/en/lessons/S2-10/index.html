Can a 14 beat a 16? The z-score

Lea scores 16 out of 20 on one test, Tom scores 14 out of 20 on another. Who did better? It depends on the mean and the spread of each test. We learn to compute a z-score, the number of standard deviations above the mean, to compare values that aren't on the same scale. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional examples, checked calculations. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

  • Compute a z-score

Prerequisites

Can a 14 beat a 16?

Lea scores sixteen out of twenty on one test. Tom scores fourteen out of twenty on a different test. Who did better? The answer isn't necessarily the one you'd expect.

A mark alone isn't enough

To judge a mark, you have to compare it with everyone else's.

Two tests, two scales

Let's look at each class. On Lea's test, the mean is twelve, with a standard deviation of four: the marks are widely spread. On Tom's test, the mean is ten, with a standard deviation of two: the marks are bunched together. Lea is four points above the mean, and so is Tom.

  • Lea's test: mean 12, standard deviation 4
  • Tom's test: mean 10, standard deviation 2
  • Lea: 4 points above. Tom: 4 points as well

4 points is little in a spread-out class, a lot in a tight one

But four points don't carry the same weight. In a class where the marks are spread out, it's an ordinary gap. In a class where they are bunched together, it's a remarkable one.

The z-score

The idea: measure the gap in standard deviations.

z-score = gap from the mean ÷ standard deviation

The z-score is the gap from the mean divided by the standard deviation. It tells you how many standard deviations a value lies above the mean, or below it if it is negative.

\[z=\dfrac{x-\bar{x}}{\sigma}\]

Lea: z = 1. Tom: z = 2

For Lea: sixteen minus twelve, four, divided by four: one. For Tom: fourteen minus ten, four, divided by two: two. Tom is two standard deviations above his class mean, Lea only one: relative to his own class, Tom's result is further from the mean.

\[z_{\text{Lea}}=\dfrac{16-12}{4}=1\]

A z-score has no unit: you can compare

A z-score has no unit: points divided by points. That's what lets you compare marks, race times, heights or salaries measured on different scales.

What the z-score doesn't do

One last point, often misunderstood.

Standardising changes the scale, not the shape

When you replace every value by its z-score, here is what changes. The mean becomes zero and the standard deviation becomes one. But, for the same data, the shape of the distribution doesn't change: a skewed distribution stays skewed. Standardising doesn't turn it into a bell curve.

  • Mean of the z-scores: 0; standard deviation: 1
  • A skewed distribution stays skewed
  • It isn't an automatic bell curve

Exercise

15 in maths (mean 12, SD 2) or 14 in philosophy (mean 10, SD 3): which mark stands out more?

Your turn. A pupil gets fifteen in maths, where the mean is twelve and the standard deviation is two. They get fourteen in philosophy, where the mean is ten and the standard deviation is three. Which mark stands out more?

Solution

Let's compute the z-scores. In maths: fifteen minus twelve, three, divided by two: one point five. In philosophy: fourteen minus ten, four, divided by three: about one point three three. The maths mark stands out more, even though the raw gap is smaller: three points against four.

  • Maths: (15 − 12) ÷ 2 = 1.5
  • Philosophy: (14 − 10) ÷ 3 ≈ 1.33
Answer : The maths mark

Key points

Key points. The z-score is the value minus the mean, divided by the standard deviation. It measures the distance from the mean in standard deviations, positive above and negative below, which lets you compare different scales. And it changes the scale, not the shape of the distribution.

  • z = (value − mean) ÷ standard deviation
  • Distance from the mean in standard deviations (negative below)
  • It changes the scale, not the shape
