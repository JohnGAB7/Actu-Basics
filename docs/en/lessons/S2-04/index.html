S2-04

What does “on average” mean?

The mean is what everyone would get if we pooled everything and shared it out equally. We compute it by hand, meet the Σ notation step by step, see why it is a balance point, then compute a weighted mean. Worked exercise: the mean of two classes is not the mean of their means. Fictional examples, checked calculations. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

Learning objectives

  • Compute a simple mean and a weighted mean
  • Read the mean as an equal share and as a balance point
  • Avoid averaging the means of groups of different sizes

Prerequisites

  • S2.01: frequencies and tables

“On average”: what does it mean?

“On average.” You read it in every article. But what exactly does it mean?

The mean, an equal share

Let's start with a simple picture: sharing.

Four friends, one shared pot

Four friends pool their money. They have five, ten, fifteen and thirty euros. In the pot, that makes sixty euros. Shared equally between the four, that is fifteen euros each: that's the mean.

  • They have €5, €10, €15 and €30
  • All in the pot: €60
  • Split four ways: €15 each

Mean = sum ÷ count

Hence the formula: the mean is the sum of the values divided by how many there are. Here, sixty divided by four: fifteen.

\text{mean}=\dfrac{\text{sum of the values}}{\text{number of values}}

The Σ notation, step by step

In textbooks, this formula is written with a scary-looking symbol. There is nothing complicated about it.

Σ means “add up”

We call the values x one, x two, x three, x four, and the mean x bar. The sigma symbol tells you to add up all the values, from the first to the n-th. Then divide that sum by n, the number of values. It is exactly the shared pot calculation.

\bar{x}=\dfrac{x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4}{4}

The balance point

A second way to see the mean: a balance point.

Deviations from the mean

Let's work out how far each value is from the mean. Five minus fifteen, minus ten; ten minus fifteen, minus five. Fifteen minus fifteen, zero; thirty minus fifteen, plus fifteen. Added together, these deviations come to exactly zero.

  • 5 − 15 = −10 and 10 − 15 = −5
  • 15 − 15 = 0 and 30 − 15 = +15
  • Sum of deviations: 0

Deviations from the mean balance out: they sum to 0

For the arithmetic mean, this is always true: the deviations from the mean add up to zero. As on a seesaw, the deviations on one side balance those on the other: the mean is the balance point. So a single large value, far away, is enough to move it.

The weighted mean

And what if the values don't all count the same?

14 in maths, weight 3; 10 in French, weight 1

A pupil gets fourteen in maths, with a weight of three, and ten in French, with a weight of one. Multiply each mark by its weight, add them up, then divide by the sum of the weights. Fourteen times three plus ten, fifty-two, divided by four: thirteen.

\dfrac{14\times 3+10\times 1}{3+1}

Without weights: 12. With them: 13

Without the weights, we would have found twelve. The weighted mean gives more weight to what matters most: here, maths.

Exercise

Class of 10 pupils: mean 12. Class of 30 pupils: mean 8. Overall mean?

Your turn. A class of ten pupils has a mean mark of twelve. A class of thirty pupils has a mean of eight. What is the overall mean of the forty pupils?

Solution

Let's work it out. The first class has ten times twelve, one hundred and twenty points; the second, thirty times eight, two hundred and forty points. Three hundred and sixty points for forty pupils: a mean of nine. Not ten, which would be the mean of the two means: the big class weighs three times as much.

  • 10 × 12 = 120 points and 30 × 8 = 240 points
  • (120 + 240) ÷ 40 = 9
Answer : 9, not 10

Key points

Key points. The mean is the sum shared out equally. Deviations from the mean balance out: it is a balance point. And to combine groups of different sizes, weight each mean by the size of its group.

  • Mean = the sum shared equally
  • Deviations from the mean balance out
  • Unequal groups: weight by group size
