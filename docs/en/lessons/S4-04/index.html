S4-04

Combinations: choosing without order

How many ways to pick two class reps from four pupils? And how many lottery tickets? With a small example where we can list them all, we see why we must divide by k factorial: each group has been counted several times. We derive the binomial coefficient, and compute the probability of a winning ticket. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional data. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

Learning objectives

  • Compute a binomial coefficient
  • Explain the division by k! as removing duplicate orderings
  • Compute the probability of a winning lottery ticket

Prerequisites

  • S4.03: the multiplication rule

2 reps in a class of 4: how many choices?

We want to pick two class reps from four pupils: Anne, Bruno, Clara, David. How many different pairs can we form?

List them all first

Let's list them all.

All possible pairs

Six pairs, not one more. And notice: order does not matter. The pair Anne-Bruno is the same as Bruno-Anne.

  • {Anne, Bruno}
  • {Anne, Clara}
  • {Anne, David}
  • {Bruno, Clara}
  • {Bruno, David}
  • {Clara, David}
  • Total: 6 pairs

Count with order, then correct

Let's try the multiplication rule.

With order, then divide

Four choices for the first rep, three for the second: twelve. But twelve is double six, because each pair has been counted twice — once in each order. We divide by two, that is, by two factorial. Six.

  • 1st rep: 4 choices
  • 2nd rep: 3 choices left
  • With order: 4 × 3 = 12
  • Each pair is counted twice (two orderings)
  • So: 12 / 2 = 6

The binomial coefficient

And here is the general rule.

C(n, k) = n! / (k! × (n−k)!)

The binomial coefficient, C(n, k), is n factorial divided by k factorial times (n − k) factorial. It counts the ways to choose k out of n, regardless of order. The k factorial in the denominator wipes out duplicate orderings.

C(n, k) = n! / (k! × (n-k)!)

In our example

In our example: four factorial over two factorial squared. Twenty-four over four: six. We recover our six pairs.

  • C(4, 2) = 4! / (2! × 2!)
  • = 24 / (2 × 2)
  • = 24 / 4 = 6

Lottery: 5 out of 49

Now an imaginary lottery, simplified rules.

Rules of our fictional lottery

  • The player picks 5 different numbers between 1 and 49
  • The draw picks 5 different numbers between 1 and 49, no order
  • No bonus number, no star box
  • Winning = matching exactly all 5 numbers drawn

Number of possible tickets

With order, forty-nine times forty-eight times forty-seven times forty-six times forty-five: about two hundred and twenty-eight million. But each ticket is counted one hundred and twenty times — five factorial — since the five numbers can appear in any order. Divide: close to one million nine hundred thousand possible tickets.

  • With order: 49 × 48 × 47 × 46 × 45
  • = 228,826,080
  • Each ticket counted 5! = 120 times
  • C(49, 5) = 228,826,080 / 120 = 1,906,884

The probability of winning

And the probability that your ticket is the right one?

P(win) = 1 / C(49, 5)

One winning ticket out of one million nine hundred and six thousand eight hundred and eighty-four: roughly one in one point nine million. As a decimal, five in ten million: 0.0000005. Very small.

P = 1 / C(49, 5)

Arrangement or combination?

Before writing, one question.

Does order matter?

If order matters, arrangement: A(n, k). If not, combination: C(n, k). The only difference is an extra k factorial in the combination's denominator. It wipes out duplicate orderings.

  • Yes → arrangement A(n, k) = n! / (n−k)!
  • No → combination C(n, k) = n! / (k! × (n−k)!)
  • C has an extra k! in the denominator

Your turn

Your turn.

Exercise

How many 2-card hands in a deck of 32?

A deck of thirty-two cards, a hand of two cards: order does not matter. How many possible hands? Pause.

Solution

Thirty-two times thirty-one, divided by two. Thirty-two times thirty-one is nine hundred and ninety-two. Divided by two: four hundred and ninety-six. So there are four hundred and ninety-six 2-card hands.

  • C(32, 2) = 32 × 31 / 2
  • = 992 / 2
  • = 496
Answer : 496 hands

Key points

Key points. A combination is a choice without order. It is computed by the binomial coefficient. And its k factorial in the denominator corrects the multiplication rule by removing duplicate orderings.

  • Combination: choose without order
  • C(n, k) = n! / (k! × (n−k)!)
  • The k! wipes out duplicate orderings
