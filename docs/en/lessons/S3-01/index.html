S3-01

Same average delay, opposite journeys: comparing groups

Two train lines have the same average delay: 5 minutes. Yet on one, you almost always arrive 4 to 6 minutes late; on the other, you're often on time, and sometimes 25 minutes late. We learn to compare whole groups with side-by-side box plots, looking at both the centre and the spread. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional examples, checked calculations. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

⏱ 3 min read✎ Written by JG↻ Updated 2026-11-09
Videos · Video in preparation

Learning objectives

  • Compare distributions with side-by-side box plots
  • Describe a difference in both centre and spread
  • Avoid drawing conclusions from two means alone

Prerequisites

  • S2.06: quartiles and box plots

Same average delay: same line?

Two train lines have exactly the same average delay: five minutes. Can we say they are just as good?

Two means aren't enough

Let's look at the last ten days on each line.

Delays over ten days (in minutes)

Here are the delays, day by day. On line A, they range from three to six minutes. On line B, trains often leave almost on time, but one day you wait eleven minutes, and another day twenty-five. Over ten days, both lines have the same average delay: five minutes.

  • Line A: 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 6
  • Line B: 0, 0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 5, 11, 25
  • Mean: 5 min for both

Side by side, the difference jumps out

Let's draw the two boxes side by side, on the same scale. Line A's box is small and tight around five minutes. Line B's box sits lower and is more stretched: the median is only two minutes, and a twenty-five-minute delay stands out as an outlier. The mean is the same; what passengers experience is not the same at all.

Line B: Q3 + 1.5 × interquartile range = 5 + 1.5 × 4 = 11. The 25-minute delay is an outlier.

Compare centre and spread

To compare two groups, always ask two questions.

Two questions for every comparison

Here is what to look at. First the centre: are the medians at the same level? Then the spread: are the boxes and whiskers the same height? Finally the extremes: does one group have outliers?

  • The centre: are the medians at the same level?
  • The spread: are the boxes the same height?
  • The extremes: are there outliers?

Two close means don't prove two groups are alike

Remember the trap: two very close means don't prove that two groups are alike. And a small difference in means, over a few days, may simply reflect the natural variability of the observations.

Delays by day of the week

Now here are the delays on a single line over several months, grouped by day of the week. Keep this picture in mind for the exercise.

Made-up data: one line's delays over several months, grouped by day of the week.

Exercise

Medians: Monday 6, Tuesday 4, Wednesday 3, Friday 7. Which day has the highest typical delay? Which is the most irregular?

Your turn. The medians are six minutes on Monday, four on Tuesday, three on Wednesday and seven on Friday. Which day has the highest typical delay? And which day is the most irregular, judging by the height of the boxes?

Solution

Let's see. The highest typical delay is on Friday, with a median of seven minutes, just ahead of Monday. For irregularity, compare the interquartile ranges: seven on Monday, four on Tuesday and Wednesday, eight on Friday. So Friday is also the most irregular day. Answer: Friday, both times.

  • Typical delay: Friday (median 7), then Monday
  • Interquartile ranges: Monday 7, Tuesday 4, Wednesday 4, Friday 8
  • Most irregular: Friday
Answer : Friday, both times

Key points

Key points. Compare whole distributions, not just two means. Put the boxes side by side, on the same scale. And look at the centre, the spread and the extremes.

  • Compare distributions, not just two means
  • Boxes side by side, same scale
  • Look at the centre, the spread and the extremes
