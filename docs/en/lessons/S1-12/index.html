A million measurements, and still wrong? Noise and bias

A scale that always adds two kilos is very consistent, and always wrong. Weighing a million people with it changes nothing. We learn to tell noise from bias and reliability from validity, and why more data reduces one but doesn't fix the other, with the real case of Google Flu Trends in 2013. Source: D. Lazer et al., Science, 2014. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

⏱ 3 min read✎ Written by JG↻ Updated 2026-11-08
Videos · Video in preparation

Learning objectives

  • Tell random error (noise) from systematic error (bias)
  • Tell reliability (measuring the same way twice) from validity (measuring the right thing)
  • Explain why more data reduces noise but doesn't fix bias

Prerequisites

  • S1.07: missing data, errors and definitions
  • S1.05: samples and selection bias

A scale that always adds 2 kilos

A scale that always adds two kilos. It gives the same result every time. Is it a good scale?

Noise and bias

To answer, we need to tell two kinds of error apart.

Two kinds of error

There are two kinds of error. Noise, or random error: the measurement is sometimes a bit too high, sometimes a bit too low, at random. Bias, or systematic error: the measurement is always wrong in the same direction.

  • Noise: random errors, sometimes too high, sometimes too low
  • Bias: always in the same direction

What the scale shows

What you read is the true value, plus the bias, plus the noise. A person weighing seventy kilos reads seventy-two on our scale: two kilos of bias, and no noise.

\[\mathrm{reading}=\mathrm{true\ value}+\mathrm{bias}+\mathrm{noise}\]

Reliable or valid?

Hence two different qualities. Reliable: a reliable instrument gives the same result when you measure the same thing twice. Valid: a valid instrument really measures what it claims to measure. Our scale is very reliable, but not valid.

  • Reliable: measures the same way twice
  • Valid: measures the right thing

More data doesn't fix everything

What if we weighed many more people?

Weighing a million people

Let's weigh a million people. If the errors are random, with no preferred direction, those up and those down tend to cancel out in the average. But the bias stays intact: the average will always be two kilos too high, very precisely.

  • The noise tends to cancel out in the average
  • The bias stays: +2 kg for everyone

The average settles… on 72 kg

Here is a simulation: thirty weighings of the same seventy-kilo person on our scale, with a little noise added. At first, the average jumps around. Then it settles. But it settles around seventy-two kilos, not seventy: the noise has shrunk, the bias has stayed.

Simulation: 30 weighings of a 70 kg person, random noise with a standard deviation of 2 kg, bias of +2 kg.

More data reduces noise, but doesn't fix bias

Remember this: more data reduces noise, but doesn't fix bias. A result can be very precise, and wrong.

A real case: flu and online searches

A real case showed this on a huge scale.

Google Flu Trends

Google Flu Trends set out to estimate flu from searches made on Google. In February twenty thirteen, the tool was predicting more than double the proportion of doctor visits for flu-like illness measured by the US health authorities.

  • Estimating flu from Google searches
  • February 2013: more than double the measured share of flu doctor visits

D. Lazer et al., “The Parable of Google Flu”, Science, 2014.

Huge data, but an indirect measure

The data were huge. But searches don't measure flu: they measure what people look for. These searches also shift with the news, people's habits and changes to the search engine. You can search for “flu” because you hear about it, without being ill. So lots of data is not enough to guarantee a valid measure.

Exercise

A fictional online survey: 2 million answers, margin of error of 0.1 point. Why is that misleading?

Your turn. A fictional online survey gets two million answers. It announces a margin of error of zero point one point. Why can this figure be misleading?

Solution

Here's the answer. The usual margin of error only measures the uncertainty of a randomly drawn sample. But online respondents aren't drawn at random: they chose to answer. If they differ from the population, the bias stays intact, whatever the size of the survey.

  • The usual margin of error assumes a random draw
  • Online respondents aren't drawn at random
Answer : The bias stays intact, whatever the size

Key takeaways

Key takeaways. Noise goes every which way, bias always the same way. A reliable instrument isn't necessarily valid. And more data reduces noise, but leaves bias untouched.

  • Noise: at random; bias: always the same way
  • Reliable isn't valid
  • More data: less noise, just as much bias
