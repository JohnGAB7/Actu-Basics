S1-10

Can “anonymous” data give you away?

Removing the name from a file isn't enough to make it anonymous. Date of birth, sex and postcode: combined, these three columns make most people unique. We see the difference between pseudonymising and anonymising, work out why every extra column isolates each row a bit more, and learn three good practices for publishing data without exposing anyone. Source: L. Sweeney, Carnegie Mellon University, 2000. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

Learning objectives

  • Explain the difference between anonymisation and pseudonymisation
  • Name one re-identification risk and one good practice to limit it

Prerequisites

  • S1.04: where data come from

No name, no address… and yet recognisable

A file with no names and no addresses. And yet, you can sometimes find a specific person in it. How?

Pseudonymous or anonymous?

First, two words that people often mix up.

Two different operations

There are two different operations. Pseudonymising means replacing the name with a code: Mary Smith becomes number four thousand and twelve, but the rest of the row stays the same. Anonymising means transforming the data so the person can no longer reasonably be found, even by combining it with other available information.

  • Pseudonymise: replace the name with a code
  • Anonymise: so the person can no longer reasonably be found

Removing the name isn't enough

So removing the name isn't enough. It all depends on what is left in the other columns.

Combining columns

Let's do the maths in a fictional town of two thousand people.

Day of birth and sex

Take the day of birth and sex. Three hundred and sixty-five days, times two: seven hundred and thirty possible combinations. For two thousand residents, that's about two point seven people per combination, on average. Such a low average signals a risk, but on its own it doesn't tell us how people are spread out.

365\times 2=730\ \mathrm{combinations}

Now add the year of birth

Now add the year of birth, with eighty possible years. That makes fifty-eight thousand four hundred combinations, for only two thousand residents. That sharply increases the risk of rare, sometimes unique, combinations. But the number of combinations alone doesn't tell us how people are really spread out.

365\times 2\times 80=58{,}400

This isn't just a hypothesis. In two thousand, the researcher Latanya Sweeney estimated, from the nineteen ninety US census, that eighty-seven percent of people in the United States were likely unique with just three pieces of information: their ZIP code, their sex and their date of birth.

The more columns you combine, the more each row becomes unique

Remember the mechanism: the more columns you combine, the more each row becomes unique. And a unique row only needs a neighbour, an article or a social network to be linked to a name.

Protecting without hiding everything

How can you publish useful data without exposing anyone?

Three good practices

Three good practices. Aggregate: publish totals by group, rather than individual rows. Generalise: give the year rather than the exact date, the region rather than the town. Suppress the boxes that contain too few people, because a box of one person is a person.

  • Aggregate: publish totals, not rows
  • Generalise: the year rather than the date
  • Suppress boxes with too few people

Exercise

An “anonymous” file of 500 staff: birth month, sex, department (10 departments). Risky?

Your turn. A so-called anonymous file describes five hundred employees, with their month of birth, their sex and their department, out of ten departments. How many possible combinations are there? Is the file risk-free?

Solution

Here's the calculation. Twelve months, times two sexes, times ten departments: two hundred and forty combinations. Five hundred divided by two hundred and forty, about two point one people per box, on average. That's a warning sign: some combinations may cover only one or two people, easy for a colleague to recognise.

  • 12 months × 2 sexes × 10 departments = 240 combinations
  • 500 ÷ 240 ≈ 2.1 people per box on average
Answer : Risk: some boxes may hold very few people

240 boxes, 500 staff placed at random

To picture the mechanism, let's simulate a uniform, random spread. Here are the two hundred and forty combinations: twelve months, times two sexes, times ten departments. We place the five hundred employees at random, each in a box. The result: sixty-one boxes hold just one person, and twenty-seven are empty.

  • 12 months × 2 sexes × 10 departments
  • Each employee lands in a box
  • 61 boxes with one person, 27 empty

Key takeaways

Key takeaways. Pseudonymising isn't anonymising. Every column you combine isolates each person a little more. And to protect people, you aggregate, you generalise, and you suppress small counts.

  • Pseudonymising isn't anonymising
  • Each combined column isolates more
  • Aggregate, generalise, suppress small counts
