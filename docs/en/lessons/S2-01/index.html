S2-01

Summing up 1,000 answers without reading them one by one

Count, then divide by the total: that is already statistics. With a fictional class of 25 pupils, we build a table of counts, frequencies and cumulative frequencies, then see why 12 can be “less” than 10. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional examples, checked calculations. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

⏱ 3 min read✎ Written by JG↻ Updated 2026-11-08
Videos · Video in preparation

Learning objectives

  • Build a table of counts and frequencies
  • Compute cumulative frequencies
  • Compare two groups of different sizes with frequencies, not counts

Prerequisites

  • S1.03: variables and types of data
  • S1.08: percentages and points

A thousand answers: how do you sum them up?

A thousand answers to a survey: nobody is going to read them one by one. To learn the method, let's start with a small class of twenty-five pupils. How do you sum up their answers?

Counting: the counts

Step one is very simple: count.

How many brothers and sisters? Class of 25

We ask twenty-five pupils how many brothers and sisters they have. Five have none, ten have one, six have two and four have three. These numbers are called counts.

Fictional class of 25 pupils.

Always check the total: 5 + 10 + 6 + 4 = 25

First habit: check the total. Five plus ten plus six plus four does make twenty-five. Without the total, a count doesn't mean much.

Dividing by the total: frequencies

Step two: divide each count by the total.

Frequency = count ÷ total

The frequency is the count divided by the total. For the pupils with one brother or sister: ten divided by twenty-five is zero point four zero, or forty percent.

\[\text{frequency}=\dfrac{\text{count}}{\text{total count}}\]

The full table

Here is the full table. No brothers or sisters: five pupils, or twenty percent. One: ten pupils, or forty percent. Two: six pupils, or twenty-four percent. Three: four pupils, or sixteen percent.

  • 0: 5 pupils, or 20%
  • 1: 10 pupils, or 40%
  • 2: 6 pupils, or 24%
  • 3: 4 pupils, or 16%

Frequencies always add up to 100%

Frequencies always add up to one hundred percent. If they don't, someone is missing, or rounding got in the way.

Adding up: cumulative frequencies

Step three: cumulative frequencies.

At most so many brothers and sisters

We add the frequencies as we go. At most zero brothers or sisters: twenty percent. At most one: twenty plus forty, sixty percent. At most two: sixty plus twenty-four, eighty-four percent. At most three: one hundred percent, the whole class.

  • At most 0: 20%
  • At most 1: 20 + 40 = 60%
  • At most 2: 60 + 24 = 84%
  • At most 3: 84 + 16 = 100%

of pupils have at most two brothers and sisters

84%

One sentence now sums up the class: eighty-four percent of the pupils have at most two brothers and sisters. Cumulating makes sense here because the values come in order.

The trap: comparing counts

One last point, and it is the most common trap.

Two classes, two sizes

Let's compare two classes. In class A, with twenty-five pupils, ten have exactly one brother or sister, or forty percent. In class B, with forty pupils, there are twelve, only thirty percent.

  • Class A, 25 pupils: 10 have one brother or sister, 40%
  • Class B, 40 pupils: 12 have one, 30%

12 is more than 10, but 30% is less than 40%

Twelve is more than ten. But relative to the size of the class, it is less. To compare groups of different sizes, compare frequencies, not counts.

Exercise

20 employees: 8 car, 6 public transport, 4 bike, 2 walk. Frequencies and cumulative?

Your turn. Twenty employees say how they get to work: eight by car, six by public transport, four by bike and two on foot. Work out the frequencies, then the cumulative frequencies.

Solution

Here's the answer. Car: eight divided by twenty, forty percent; public transport: thirty percent. Bike: twenty percent; walking: ten percent. Adding up in this order: forty, seventy, ninety, then one hundred percent. Careful: ways of getting to work have no natural order. The cumulative figures depend on the order chosen, here from most to least common.

  • Car 8 ÷ 20 = 40%, public transport 6 ÷ 20 = 30%
  • Bike 4 ÷ 20 = 20%, walk 2 ÷ 20 = 10%
  • Cumulative, from car to walking: 40, 70, 90, 100%
Answer : Total 100%, cumulative in the chosen order

Key points

Key points. Count, then check the total. Divide by the total to compare groups of different sizes. And only cumulate when the values have an order, or once you have chosen one.

  • Count, then check the total
  • Divide by the total to compare
  • Cumulate only when the values have an order
