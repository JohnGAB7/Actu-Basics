$1,000 today or in 30 years: is it really the same money?

Would you rather have $1,000 today or $1,000 in 30 years? Same number, very different value. In this episode of our actuarial science series, we build the most basic tool of the actuary: compound interest and discounting. We see why $1,000 due in 30 years is worth about $231 today at 5% a year, why the interest rate matters so much to an insurer, and how to do it all in a spreadsheet. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional rates, checked calculations, no investment advice.

$1,000 today or $1,000 in 30 years?

One thousand dollars today, or one thousand dollars in thirty years. Same number. But are they really the same money? For an actuary, certainly not.

Insurers receive money now and pay it later

An insurer collects premiums today and pays benefits later: next month for a stolen phone, decades later for a pension. To compare money paid at different dates, actuaries use one tool again and again: interest. One warning before we start: this episode is about the time value of money, not about inflation or what money can buy. In this episode, we build it from scratch: simple interest, compound interest, and then the key idea, discounting.

Simple or compound interest

Let's start with a deposit that earns interest.

$1,000 at 5% a year, simple interest

Imagine you deposit one thousand dollars at five percent a year, with simple interest. Each year, you earn five percent of one thousand dollars, that is fifty dollars. After thirty years, that makes thirty times fifty, or one thousand five hundred dollars of interest. In total, you have two thousand five hundred dollars.

  • Each year: 5% of $1,000 = $50
  • After 30 years: 30 × $50 = $1,500 of interest
  • Total: $2,500

Fictional, constant rate. Simple interest: interest is always computed on the original $1,000.

Compound interest: interest earns interest

With compound interest, the interest is added to the balance, and then earns interest itself. After one year, one thousand times one point zero five gives one thousand and fifty dollars. In the second year, you earn five percent on one thousand and fifty, which gives one thousand one hundred and two dollars fifty. In other words, each year, the balance is multiplied by one point zero five.

  • Year 1: $1,000 × 1.05 = $1,050
  • Year 2: $1,050 × 1.05 = $1,102.50
  • Each year, multiply by 1.05

The accumulation factor

After n years, the balance has been multiplied by one plus i, n times: one plus i to the power n. Here i is the constant effective annual interest rate, with interest added once a year; PV is the amount you start with, and FV the future value. With one thousand dollars at five percent for thirty years, we get about four thousand three hundred and twenty-two dollars.

\[FV=PV\times(1+i)^n\]

$1,000 at 5% a year, compound interest

Here is how the balance grows. After ten years, about one thousand six hundred and twenty-nine dollars. After twenty years, about two thousand six hundred and fifty-three. After thirty years, about four thousand three hundred and twenty-two: far more than the two thousand five hundred of simple interest.

[Chart for this step]

Rule of thumb: money doubles in about 72 ÷ rate years

A handy rule of thumb: at a rate of r percent, money roughly doubles in seventy-two divided by r years. At five percent, seventy-two divided by five gives fourteen point four years. The exact answer is about fourteen point two years: close enough for a quick check.

Discounting: going back in time

Now let's turn the question around.

How much must I invest today to have $1,000 in 30 years?

Compound interest takes money forward in time. Actuaries often need the opposite. JG's Holding, our fictional insurer, promises to pay one thousand dollars in thirty years. How much must it set aside today, at five percent a year, to be able to pay it?

The present value

We simply divide instead of multiplying. The present value is the future value divided by one plus i to the power n. Actuaries write v for one divided by one plus i: it is called the discount factor. Here, one thousand divided by one point zero five to the power thirty gives about two hundred and thirty-one dollars and thirty-eight cents.

\[PV=\dfrac{FV}{(1+i)^n}=FV\times v^n\]

Today's value of $1,000 paid later, at 5%

The further away the payment, the less it is worth today. One thousand dollars paid in ten years is worth about six hundred and fourteen dollars today. Paid in twenty years, about three hundred and seventy-seven. Paid in thirty years, only about two hundred and thirty-one.

[Chart for this step]

At 5%, today's $231 grows into tomorrow's $1,000

This does not mean that the thousand dollars has lost value by magic. It means that, under our assumed five percent annual rate, and ignoring taxes, inflation and default risk, two hundred and thirty-one dollars and thirty-eight cents today grow into one thousand dollars in thirty years. Discounting is just compound interest read backwards.

Why the rate matters so much

Here is why actuaries argue so much about interest rates.

$1,000 in 30 years: value today

Let's change only the interest rate. At two percent a year, one thousand dollars in thirty years is worth about five hundred and fifty-two dollars today. At five percent, about two hundred and thirty-one. At eight percent, only about ninety-nine dollars.

  • At 2%: about $552
  • At 5%: about $231
  • At 8%: about $99

Fictional rates, kept constant for 30 years. Real rates change over time.

A lower rate means a higher present value of future payments

For an insurer with long-term promises, this is a huge deal. All else being equal, using a lower discount rate increases the present value of its future payments. This can increase the amount it needs to recognise or set aside, depending on the valuation framework that applies to it. Over thirty years, moving from five percent to two percent more than doubles the present value. That is why the rate is never chosen freely: it is set by rules, stated, justified and checked.

In a spreadsheet

You can do all of this in any spreadsheet.

Three spreadsheet formulas

Here are three formulas to try. For the future value, the built-in function F V, with a rate of five percent, thirty years and one thousand dollars, returns about four thousand three hundred and twenty-two dollars. For the present value, you can divide by hand: one thousand divided by one plus five percent, to the power thirty. Or you can use the built-in present value function, P V, which gives the same two hundred and thirty-one dollars and thirty-eight cents.

  • Future value: =FV(5%, 30, 0, -1000)
  • Present value by hand: =1000/(1+5%)^30
  • Present value, built in: =PV(5%, 30, 0, -1000)

FV and PV are built-in functions in most spreadsheets. The minus sign is a cash-flow convention.

Exercise

JG's Holding must pay $10,000 in 10 years. At 4% a year, how much must it set aside today?

Your turn. JG's Holding must pay ten thousand dollars in ten years. At four percent a year, how much must it set aside today?

Solution

Let's work it out. One point zero four to the power ten is about one point four eight zero two. We divide ten thousand dollars by this factor. As a check, the amount we find, multiplied again by one point zero four to the power ten, gives back ten thousand dollars. JG's Holding must set aside about six thousand seven hundred and fifty-five dollars and sixty-four cents today.

  • Discount factor: 1.04^10 ≈ 1.4802
  • Present value: $10,000 ÷ 1.4802
  • Check: $6,755.64 × 1.04^10 ≈ $10,000
Answer : About $6,755.64 today

Key takeaways

Key takeaways. With compound interest, the balance is multiplied by one plus i each year. The future value is the present value times one plus i to the power n. The present value is the future value divided by one plus i to the power n. And the lower the interest rate, the higher the present value of a future payment.

  • Compound interest: multiply by (1 + i) each year
  • Future value: PV × (1 + i)^n
  • Present value: FV ÷ (1 + i)^n
  • A lower rate means a higher present value
