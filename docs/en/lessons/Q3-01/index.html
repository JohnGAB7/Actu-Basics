Q3-01

Why your bus always seems late

A bus every 10 minutes on average: you'd expect to wait 5 minutes on average. But if buses run irregularly, the average wait goes above 6 minutes. Why? You're more likely to turn up during a long gap than a short one. It's the inspection paradox, and it also shows up in class sizes and waiting lines. All numbers checked; assumption: you arrive at the stop at a random moment.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the inspection paradox: you are more likely to land in a long gap
  • Work out the average wait when the gaps between buses are 5 and 15 minutes
  • Spot other situations where the same bias shows up

Prerequisites

  • S2.04: the mean
  • Reading a fraction

A bus every 10 minutes on average. So why do you wait more than 5?

A bus comes every ten minutes on average. So you should wait five minutes on average. Why does it so often feel like longer?

If buses are perfectly regular, the average wait really is 5 minutes

Let's start with the simple case. If a bus comes exactly every ten minutes, and you turn up at a random moment, you wait anywhere from zero to ten minutes. On average, five minutes: half the gap.

But buses aren't regular

In real life, buses catch up with each other and bunch together. Let's say they alternate: first a gap of five minutes. Then a gap of fifteen minutes. And so on. The average gap is still ten minutes.

  • A gap of 5 minutes
  • Then a gap of 15 minutes
  • And so on: still 10 minutes on average

CHALLENGE

Buses every 5, then 15 minutes. Is the average wait shorter, the same or longer than 5 minutes?

What do you think? With these irregular buses, is the average wait shorter than five minutes, the same, or longer?

Where do you land?

To answer, ask yourself which gap you're likely to turn up in.

Out of 20 minutes, the long gap takes up 15

Look at a twenty-minute stretch: one short gap, then one long gap. If you turn up at random, you land in the short gap with a probability of five out of twenty, or twenty-five percent. And in the long gap with a probability of fifteen out of twenty, or seventy-five percent.

  • Short gap: 5 min out of 20, a 25% chance
  • Long gap: 15 min out of 20, a 75% chance

Long gaps last longer, so you're more likely to land in one

That's the whole paradox. There are as many long gaps as short ones. But the long ones last longer, so you're far more likely to turn up during one of them.

The average wait

Now let's work out the average wait.

Within a gap, you wait half of it on average

Once you're in a gap, you wait half of it on average. In a five-minute gap, two and a half minutes. In a fifteen-minute gap, seven and a half minutes.

  • Short gap: a 2.5-minute wait
  • Long gap: a 7.5-minute wait

Weight each by its chance

Combine the two, each with its own chance. A twenty-five percent chance of waiting two and a half minutes, plus a seventy-five percent chance of waiting seven and a half. That's zero point six two five, plus five point six two five: six and a quarter minutes.

\[0.25\times 2.5+0.75\times 7.5\]

average wait, instead of 5 minutes

6.25 min

So here's the answer: the average wait is longer. Six and a quarter minutes instead of five, with the same number of buses. The average timetable hasn't changed. Only the regularity has.

The more irregular the buses, the longer you wait

And the more irregular the buses, the longer the average wait. Now suppose buses arrive according to a Poisson process, with an average of ten minutes between arrivals. The time between arrivals then follows an exponential distribution. In this model, even though the average time between buses is ten minutes, a person arriving at a randomly chosen time also waits ten minutes on average. That's the subject of another video, on the exponential distribution.

Exercise

Now the gaps are 2 and 18 minutes. What's the average wait?

Your turn. The buses now alternate gaps of two minutes and eighteen minutes. The average gap is still ten minutes. What's the average wait?

Solution

You land in the short gap with a ten percent chance, and in the long gap with a ninety percent chance. The average waits there are one minute and nine minutes. Zero point one times one, plus zero point nine times nine: eight point two. The average wait is eight minutes and twelve seconds.

  • Chances: 2 out of 20 = 10%; 18 out of 20 = 90%
  • Waits: 1 min and 9 min
  • 0.1 × 1 + 0.9 × 9 = 8.2
Answer : 8.2 minutes on average

Beyond the bus stop

This bias shows up whenever we see the world through what we happen to experience.

The inspection paradox is everywhere

The same mechanism turns up in many other places. If we choose a class at random, every class has the same chance of being selected; but if we choose a student at random, larger classes are more likely to be represented, because they contain more students. Drop by a gym at a random moment, and you'll mostly meet the people who go often. And if you survey the patients in a hospital on a given day, you'll mostly find long illnesses, because those patients stay longer.

  • A student picked at random is more often in a big class
  • Drop by a gym at random: you mostly meet the regulars
  • Survey patients on a given day: you mostly find long illnesses

Key takeaways

Let's recap. One: turning up at random, you land in long gaps more often. Two: for the average wait, each gap counts according to its length. Three: for the same average gap, the more irregular the buses, the longer you wait.

  • You land in long gaps more often
  • Average wait: weight each gap by its length
  • Irregular = longer wait, for the same average
