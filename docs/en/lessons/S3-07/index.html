S3-07

Drawing a line through the cloud: least squares

In a scatter plot, how do we pick "the right" line? With twelve imaginary pupils and three candidate lines, we make the squared errors visible and discover the least squares criterion. We use it for a careful prediction, and we spell out what the line does not yet prove. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional data. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

⏱ 4 min read✎ Written by JG↻ Updated 2026-11-13
Videos · Video in preparation

Learning objectives

  • Explain the least squares criterion with visible squares
  • Use a line for a careful prediction, inside the range of the observed data
  • State three questions the line does not yet answer

Prerequisites

  • S3.05: correlation

Among three lines, which one fits best?

We have a scatter plot, and we would like to summarise it with a line. But among all the possible lines, how do we choose?

The cloud of our twelve pupils

Let us start by placing the cloud.

Hours of revision and marks, 12 fictional pupils

Twelve imaginary pupils. On the horizontal axis, hours of revision; on the vertical axis, the mark out of twenty. The cloud goes up: more hours goes with better marks. We would now like to draw a line through it.

Imaginary pupils: numbers made up for the example.

The line and its two numbers

A line is described by two numbers: where it starts and how steep it is.

y = a + b x

Y equals a plus b times x. The number a is the intercept: the height of the line when x is zero. The number b is the slope: how much y goes up when x increases by one unit.

\[y = a + b x\]

Which criterion?

Among all the possible lines, we want one that is, on average, close to the points.

For each point, measure the vertical error

For each point, we measure the vertical distance between the point and the line. We square it, so that an error below and an error above both count positively. We add them up over the twelve pupils. And among all possible lines, we keep the one that makes this sum smallest. This is called least squares.

  • Take the vertical gap between the point and the line
  • Square it, so it always counts positively
  • Add them up over all the points
  • Pick the line that makes this sum smallest

Three candidates, three sums

Let us try three candidate lines.

Sum of squared errors for three lines

Three equations, three sums. Line A gives twenty-nine. Line B, a bit steeper, gives eight point seven six: three times less. Line C, which aims too high, is very poor: eighty-seven. Among these three candidates, line B wins. But it is not the best line overall: only the best among these three.

  • A · y = 6 + x · sum 29.0
  • B · y = 6 + 1.2 x · sum 8.76
  • C · y = 4 + 2 x · sum 87.0

The actual least-squares line

The exact solution, computed on the twelve pupils, is roughly: y equals six point three six plus one point one eight times x. Its sum of squares is seven point seven eight, a bit less than line B. Line B is therefore not the solution; it is a good approximation of it.

  • Computed on the twelve points: y ≈ 6.36 + 1.18 x
  • Sum of squares: 7.78
  • Line B approximates this solution; it is not equal to it

Predicting, but carefully

Once the line is chosen, we can use it to predict.

Predict with care

Predict, yes, but inside the observed range: between one and nine hours of revision. Outside, you extrapolate, and nothing guarantees the relation stays linear. Even inside, every prediction has an error: the line is a summary, not an oracle. In 1886, Francis Galton drew such a line between parents' and children's heights, and noticed that the children of very tall parents were, on average, slightly less tall: this is regression towards the mean.

  • Predict inside the observed range (here, 1 to 9 hours)
  • Not outside: the line may no longer fit
  • Remember that every prediction has an error

Three questions the line does not answer

And before we conclude, three questions the line does not answer.

The line does not yet tell you

One: the line could come from sampling chance. With twelve other pupils, we would get another line. Two: a relation is not necessarily causal. The line does not say that revising more makes the mark go up. Three: it holds inside the observed range, and not further.

  • Is it due to sampling chance? (will be seen in S6)
  • Is the relation causal? (covered in a later module)
  • Does it hold outside the observed data?

Your turn

Your turn.

Three new lines, four points

Four points this time. P1: zero, two. P2: one, three. P3: two, six. P4: three, five. And three candidate lines.

  • P1: (0, 2)
  • P2: (1, 3)
  • P3: (2, 6)
  • P4: (3, 5)

Exercise

Among y = 2 + x, y = 1.5 + 1.5 x and y = 1 + 2 x, which has the smallest sum of squared errors?

Y equals two plus x. Y equals one point five plus one point five x. Y equals one plus two x. For each, compute the sum of squared vertical errors on the four points, and say which one wins.

Solution

For the first line, y equals two plus x, the predictions are two, three, four, five. The gaps with the observations are zero, zero, minus two, zero. Sum of squares: four. For the second, y equals one point five plus one point five x: predictions one point five, three, four point five, six. Gaps: minus zero point five, zero, minus one point five, one. Squares: zero point two five, zero, two point two five, one. Sum: three point five. For the third, y equals one plus two x: predictions one, three, five, seven. Gaps: minus one, zero, minus one, two. Squares: one, zero, one, four. Sum: six. The middle line wins.

  • y = 2 + x : 4.0
  • y = 1.5 + 1.5 x : 3.5
  • y = 1 + 2 x : 6.0
Answer : The middle line, y = 1.5 + 1.5 x, wins with 3.5

Key points

Key points. Least squares: the line that minimises the sum of squared vertical errors. We use it to predict, inside the observed range, and not further. And a line, however neat, does not prove a cause.

  • Least squares: the line that minimises the sum of squares
  • Predict inside the observed range only
  • The line does not prove a cause
