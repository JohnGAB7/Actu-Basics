“80% of crashes involve sober drivers”: what does that number really say?

"80% of crashes involve a driver who hadn't been drinking." Does that mean drinking protects you? Of course not: almost all drivers are sober. With a made-up cross-tab of 100,000 drivers, we learn to compute row and column percentages, and to pick the right denominator. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional examples, checked calculations. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

80% of crashes: a sober driver. What does it say?

Imagine this headline: eighty percent of crashes involve a driver who hadn't been drinking. Should we conclude that it's better to drink before driving? Of course not: let's see what that number really says.

The cross-tab

To answer, we need to combine two pieces of information.

100,000 imaginary drivers

Take a hundred thousand imaginary drivers over one year. Ninety-eight thousand are sober, and four hundred of them have a crash. Two thousand have been drinking, and one hundred of them have a crash. Of the five hundred crashes, four hundred involve a sober driver: eighty percent. The headline is accurate.

  • Sober: 98,000, of whom 400 crash
  • Had been drinking: 2,000, of whom 100 crash
  • Crashes: 500, of which 400 sober, so 80%

Numbers invented for the example: these are not road safety statistics.

Two variables, four cells

This is a cross-tab: each driver is sorted by two variables at once. In the rows, sober or not; in the columns, crash or not. That gives four cells.

  • Sober, crash: 400 · Sober, no crash: 97,600
  • Drinking, crash: 100 · Drinking, no crash: 1,900

Out of whom?

Everything depends on one question: out of whom do we compute the percentage?

Two percentages, two questions

We can divide by two different totals. Among crashes, eighty percent involve sober drivers: that's the headline figure. But among sober drivers, only four hundred out of ninety-eight thousand have a crash: about zero point four one percent. And among those who have been drinking, one hundred out of two thousand: five percent.

  • Among crashes: 80% sober
  • Among sober drivers: 400 ÷ 98,000 ≈ 0.41% crash
  • Among drinkers: 100 ÷ 2,000 = 5% crash

Share who crash: about 12 times higher

The question that matters for a driver is the share of drivers in their group who have a crash. In this fictional example, it's about five percent after drinking, against zero point four one percent when sober: about twelve times higher. Sober drivers make up most crashes simply because they make up almost all drivers. This fictional table illustrates the choice of denominator: on its own, it doesn't prove cause and effect.

\[\dfrac{5\%}{0.41\%}\approx 12\]

Column or row percentage: pick the right denominator

The classic trap is to read a percentage computed among crashes as if it were computed among drivers. Before you conclude anything, always ask: percent of whom?

Exercise

200 smokers, of whom 60 cough. 800 non-smokers, of whom 80 cough. What share of each group coughs?

Your turn. In an imaginary company, two hundred smokers, sixty of whom cough every morning. Eight hundred non-smokers, eighty of whom cough. What share of each group coughs?

Solution

Let's compute. Among smokers, sixty out of two hundred: thirty percent. Among non-smokers, eighty out of eight hundred: ten percent. And if we switch denominators, of the hundred and forty people who cough, sixty smoke: about forty-three percent, a minority. Yet the risk of coughing is three times higher for smokers: thirty percent against ten.

  • Smokers: 60 ÷ 200 = 30%
  • Non-smokers: 80 ÷ 800 = 10%
  • Among those who cough, 60 of 140 smoke: ≈ 43%
Answer : 30% versus 10%

Key points

Key points. A cross-tab sorts everyone by two variables. A percentage depends on its denominator, the group it is computed within. So always ask: percent of whom?

  • A cross-tab sorts everyone by two variables
  • A percentage depends on its denominator
  • Always ask: percent of whom?
