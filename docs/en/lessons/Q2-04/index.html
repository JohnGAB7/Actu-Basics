What do the returning planes tell us?

During the Second World War, analysts examined the damage on aircraft returning from missions. But the planes that were lost are missing from the data: a filter, survival, sorted what could be observed. The statistician Abraham Wald proposed a method that accounts for it. This is survivorship bias, and it also hides behind the recipes of successful companies. The numbers in the animation are made up. Sources: A. Wald, "A Method of Estimating Plane Vulnerability Based on Damage of Survivors", Center for Naval Analyses reprint (CRC 432), 1980; M. Mangel and F. J. Samaniego, "Abraham Wald's Work on Aircraft Survivability", Journal of the American Statistical Association, 1984. To go further: the lesson on samples and selection bias.

We only examine the planes that came back. What can we conclude?

During the Second World War, analysts examined the damage on aircraft returning from missions. But what could they really conclude from it?

An invisible filter

Imagine a mission, with made-up numbers. Eight planes set out. Five come back, and three are missing. The analysts can only examine the five survivors. Between the planes that set out and the data, a filter was at work: survival.

  • Imagine 8 planes on a mission
  • 5 come back, 3 are missing
  • The data contain only the survivors
  • In between: selection by survival

Abraham Wald's method

The statistician Abraham Wald proposed a method for this problem. It starts from the planes that returned, sorted by the number of hits they took. And it accounts for the total number of planes sent out, to estimate what happened to the missing ones.

  • Start from the returning planes, by their number of hits
  • Account for the total number of planes sent out

A. Wald, CNA reprint (CRC 432), 1980.

The same filter: companies

The same filter works elsewhere. Companies are founded. Many disappear along the way. And we only hear the survivors share their recipes, even though the ones that closed may have done exactly the same.

  • Companies are founded
  • Many disappear
  • We only hear from the survivors

Survivorship bias

This is survivorship bias: drawing conclusions from the cases that passed a selection filter while overlooking those that disappeared before they could be observed.

Exercise

A study only looks at companies still in business. Which group is missing?

Solution

They were removed before anyone could observe them. They're the companies that closed.

  • Removed before anyone looked
