“A one-in-ten chance”: what does it mean?

A one-in-ten chance doesn't mean “on the tenth try”. We learn to write a chance in three ways, fraction, frequency and percentage, to see why 60 throws of a die almost never give exactly 10 sixes, and why an event that is rare for each person happens to someone every day. Fictional examples, checked calculations. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

Learning objectives

  • Express a chance as a fraction, a frequency and a percentage
  • Explain with an example why two samples from the same population give different results
  • Tell “rare” from “impossible”

Prerequisites

  • S1.08: percentages and points

“A one-in-ten chance”: what does it mean?

A one-in-ten chance. You hear it everywhere. But what does it actually mean?

One chance, three ways to write it

The same chance can be written in three ways.

A one-in-ten chance is…

Here are the three. A fraction: one in ten. A frequency: out of a thousand similar situations, it happens about a hundred times. A percentage: ten percent.

  • A fraction: 1 in 10
  • A frequency: 100 times out of 1,000 similar situations
  • A percentage: 10%

The frequency is the most telling

The frequency is often the most telling. Picture a thousand people in the same situation: how many of them will experience the event?

One in ten doesn't mean “on the tenth try”

Watch out for a common misreading: a one-in-ten chance doesn't mean it will happen on the tenth try.

chance of getting it at least once in 10 independent tries

65{'fr': ' %', 'en': '%'}

In ten independent tries, you have about a sixty-five percent chance of getting it at least once. That leaves a thirty-five percent chance of never getting it. If the tries are independent, earlier failures don't change the chance of the next one.

Chance in the data

Chance isn't only in games. It's also in every number taken from a sample.

Throwing a fair die 60 times

Let's throw a fair die sixty times. On each throw, a six has a one-in-six chance. On average, we expect ten sixes, since sixty divided by six is ten. But in practice, we get eight, eleven, thirteen: rarely exactly ten.

  • We expect 10 sixes on average
  • But we get 8, 11, 13…

10 series of 60 throws: how many sixes? (expected: 10)

Here is a simulation: ten series of sixty throws. One series gives nine sixes; another, sixteen; another, four. Only one series, highlighted, lands exactly on ten. Yet they all come from the same fair die.

chance of getting exactly 10 sixes

13.7{'fr': ' %', 'en': '%'}

The probability of getting exactly ten sixes is only about thirteen point seven percent. In about eighty-six percent of series of sixty throws, you get a different number of sixes.

Two polls on the same day: two different results

It's the same with a poll. Two polling firms each ask a thousand people on the same day, in the same population: they get slightly different results. That's not necessarily a mistake: it's sampling variation.

Rare isn't impossible

One last idea: rare doesn't mean impossible.

A one-in-a-million risk, in a country of 60 million

Take an event that affects one person in a million each year, in a fictional country of sixty million people. Sixty million times one millionth: we expect about sixty cases a year. The risk is tiny for each person, but across the country we expect cases every year.

60{,}000{,}000\times\dfrac{1}{1{,}000{,}000}=60

Exercise

1 risk in 500, 0.2%, 2 per 1,000, 20 in 10,000: which one is different?

Your turn. Four phrases: a one-in-five-hundred risk, zero point two percent, two per thousand, twenty in ten thousand. Which one is different from the others?

Solution

Let's work it out. One divided by five hundred is zero point zero zero two, or zero point two percent. Two per thousand and twenty in ten thousand give exactly the same. None of them is different: all four equal zero point two percent. Don't be impressed by the biggest number.

  • 1 ÷ 500 = 0.002 = 0.2%
  • 2 ÷ 1,000 = 20 ÷ 10,000 = 0.2%
Answer : None: all four equal 0.2%

Key takeaways

Key takeaways. Read a chance as a frequency: how many times out of a thousand? Every number taken from a sample contains some chance. And an event that's rare for each person often happens to someone.

  • Read a chance as a frequency
  • Every sample contains some chance
  • Rare for each, common for all
