A value that sticks out: should you delete it?

A height of 17 metres in one file, an income of 10 million euros in another: two outliers, two very different stories. We learn to flag an outlier with the 1.5 × interquartile range rule, then to decide: correct it, keep it, or analyse it separately. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional examples, checked calculations. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

Learning objectives

A value that sticks out: should you delete it?

In your data, one value sticks out above all the others. Should you delete it? Not so fast.

Flagging an outlier

First, we need a rule to say that a value sticks out.

Eleven heights in a file (in metres)

Here are eleven heights, sorted, from a file. The first quartile is one point six five metres, the median one point seven two, the third quartile one point eight zero. So the interquartile range is fifteen centimetres.

  • 1.58 · 1.62 · 1.65 · 1.68 · 1.70 · 1.72 · 1.74 · 1.76 · 1.80 · 1.85 · 17
  • Q1 = 1.65; median = 1.72; Q3 = 1.80
  • Interquartile range: 1.80 − 1.65 = 0.15 m

The 1.5 × interquartile range rule

A common rule, proposed by the statistician John Tukey, draws two fences. At the top: the third quartile plus one and a half times the interquartile range, just over two metres. At the bottom: the first quartile minus one and a half times the interquartile range, just over one point four two metres. Any value outside these fences is flagged as an outlier.

\text{top}: Q_3+1.5\times 0.15=2.025\ \text{m}

A single value squashes the whole chart

Seventeen metres is way beyond the fence. On the box plot, that lone point squashes everything else into the bottom of the chart.

Mistake or message?

A rule flags. It doesn't decide.

Case 1: 17 metres

First case: a height of seventeen metres. A human height of seventeen metres is biologically impossible. It is most likely a typing error, a missing decimal point: one point seven zero metres. We check the source, correct it, and record the correction.

  • Impossible for a human being
  • Probably a missing decimal point: 1.70
  • Action: check and correct, and record it

Case 2: an income of 10 million euros

Second case: ten yearly incomes. Nine are between eighteen thousand and thirty-five thousand euros, and the tenth reaches ten million. It's rare, but possible: in this fictional example, it belongs to the head of a company. We keep it, and choose measures that cope with extremes, such as the median.

  • Nine incomes between €18,000 and €35,000, and one of 10 million
  • Rare, but possible: a company boss
  • Action: keep it, and choose robust measures

Deleting a value to improve a result: never

The trap is to delete a value because it spoils the result. Remove the high incomes, and you invent a population that is more equal than it really is. A strange value is first of all a question to ask.

YOUR TURN

What would you do? A: a 250-year-old patient. B: 42 °C on a heatwave day. C: a €3,000 company order among individual shoppers' purchases.

Your turn. For each outlier, choose: correct, keep, or analyse separately. A: a two-hundred-and-fifty-year-old patient in a hospital file. B: forty-two degrees on a heatwave day, in a series of summer temperatures. C: a three-thousand-euro order placed by a company, among purchases by individual shoppers.

Solution

Let's see. A is impossible: check the source and correct it if the error is confirmed; otherwise, treat it as a missing value, and record that. B is real, and it's exactly what we want to know: keep it. C is real, but it isn't an individual shopper: analyse it separately. So: A correct, B keep, C separate.

  • A: impossible, check the source; otherwise, missing value, recorded
  • B: real, it is the message: keep it
  • C: real, but a different population: analyse separately
Answer : A correct, B keep, C separate

Key points

Key points. A value is flagged as an outlier beyond the quartiles, plus or minus one and a half times the interquartile range. The rule flags, it doesn't decide. Correct, keep or analyse separately: always justify your choice.

  • Outlier: beyond Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or Q3 + 1.5 × IQR
  • The rule flags, it doesn't decide
  • Correct, keep or analyse separately: always give a reason
