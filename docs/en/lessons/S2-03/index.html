S2-03

What a histogram's shape tells you before any calculation

One peak in the middle, a long tail on one side, two peaks: the shape of a histogram already tells a story. With three fictional examples, marks, incomes and heights, we learn to describe a distribution and to be wary of a single number when there are two peaks. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional examples. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

⏱ 3 min read✎ Written by JG↻ Updated 2026-11-08
Videos · Video in preparation

Learning objectives

  • Describe a distribution: symmetry, skew, one or two peaks, tail
  • Link a shape to a plausible explanation
  • Spot when a single number is not enough to sum up data

Prerequisites

  • S2.02: bar charts and histograms

Before any calculation, look at the shape

Before you calculate anything, look at the shape of a histogram. It already tells you a lot.

Symmetric: two similar sides

First shape, the simplest one.

Marks of 20 pupils, out of 20

Here are the fictional marks of twenty pupils. One peak in the middle, between eight and twelve, and two sides that mirror each other. In this example, the distribution is roughly symmetric. Here, the middle sums up the group well.

Fictional marks of 20 pupils.

Skewed: a long tail

Second shape: the skewed distribution.

Monthly incomes of 25 people

Here are fictional monthly incomes. Most people are on the left, between one thousand and three thousand euros. Then a long tail stretches to the right: a few very high incomes. This is called right skew.

Fictional incomes of 25 people, classes of €1,000.

A tail, on one side or the other

A tail can go either way. On the right, it points to a few very large values, like high incomes. On the left, a few very small values. And the tail pulls the mean towards it: we'll see that in the episode on the median.

  • Tail on the right: a few very large values
  • Tail on the left: a few very small values

Two peaks: two groups?

Third shape, the most surprising one.

Heights of 44 adults

Here are the fictional heights of forty-four adults. Two peaks: one around one hundred and sixty centimetres, the other around one hundred and seventy-five. We call this distribution bimodal. One possible explanation: two groups with different average heights have been mixed, here women and men.

Fictional heights of 44 adults, women and men mixed.

the mean falls in the dip, between the two peaks

170 cm

Using the class midpoints, we estimate the mean height at about one hundred and seventy centimetres. It falls in the dip between the two peaks, where there are few people. A single number describes neither the women nor the men in this group.

Two peaks? Check whether several groups are mixed

Keep this habit: two peaks often point to two groups mixed together. Before summing up, separate them.

Exercise

Shapes A, B, C, D: age at death, income, exam marks, heights?

Your turn. Here are four shapes, A, B, C and D. And four variables: age at death, income, exam marks, adult heights. Match each variable to its shape.

Solution

Here's the answer. Exam marks, shape B: often roughly symmetric. Income, shape D: a right tail, because of a few very high incomes. Age at death, shape A: where most deaths happen at an old age, a tail towards younger ages. Adult heights, shape C: two peaks, women and men. Behind every shape there is an explanation.

  • Exam marks: B, roughly symmetric
  • Income: D, right tail
  • Age at death: A, left tail
  • Adult heights: C, two peaks
Answer : Every shape has an explanation

Key points

Key points. Balanced sides: the distribution is roughly symmetric. A long tail: a few extreme values, on one side. Two peaks: often two groups mixed together, which a single number cannot sum up.

  • Balanced sides: roughly symmetric
  • A tail: a few extreme values
  • Two peaks: often two groups
