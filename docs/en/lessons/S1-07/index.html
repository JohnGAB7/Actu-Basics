S1-07

Bike theft reports up 51%: did thefts really increase?

In a fictional town, reported bike thefts jump 51% in one year. Did thefts go up, or did the way of counting them change? The year of the jump, online reporting opened. We learn to spot a break in a series, to be wary of missing answers (the people who don't reply are not always a random few) and to recognise data-entry errors. Fictional examples, checked calculations. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

⏱ 3 min read✎ Written by JG↻ Updated 2026-11-08
Videos · Video in preparation

Learning objectives

  • Spot a break in a series: a change of definition or method
  • Explain why missing data are not always missing at random
  • Recognise the most common data-entry errors

Prerequisites

  • S1.04: where data come from

Bike theft reports: +51% in one year

In one town, reported bike thefts jump by fifty-one percent in a single year. Did thefts really go up? Before answering, let's ask what else might have changed.

When the measurement changes

Let's look at the series more closely. The town is fictional, but the mechanism is very real.

Reported bike thefts, fictional town

For four years, around four hundred reports a year. Then six hundred and twenty in twenty twenty-three. That year, the town opened online crime reporting. No more trip to the police station.

Fictional data. 2022: 410 reports; 2023: 620.

What the residents' survey says

A survey of residents, run the same way every year, shows no rise in the number of victims. What rose is mainly the share of victims who report the theft. Before, many didn't bother. In our example, everything points to a change in recording, more than in thefts.

  • Reported victims: stable from year to year
  • What changed: the share of victims who report it

A break in the series: before and after aren't directly comparable

This is called a break in the series. When the definition or the counting method changes, the figures before and after no longer measure exactly the same thing. Statistics offices flag this in their methodology notes. A sudden jump in the year of a reform should always raise suspicion.

Missing data

Second trap: the data that are missing.

A fictional income survey

A fictional survey asks one hundred households about their income. Eighty reply, with an average income of two thousand five hundred euros. But the twenty who didn't reply aren't just anyone: they're mostly the better-off households, with five thousand euros on average.

  • 100 households asked, 80 reply: average income €2,500
  • The 20 who don't reply are mostly the better-off: €5,000

The true average of the 100 households

The true average of the hundred households is eighty times two thousand five hundred, plus twenty times five thousand, divided by one hundred: three thousand euros. Keeping only those who replied underestimates it by five hundred euros, about seventeen percent.

\[\dfrac{80\times 2{,}500+20\times 5{,}000}{100}=3{,}000\ \mathrm{€}\]

A gap in the data tells a story

Missing data aren't always an accident. If the people who don't reply differ from those who do, the result is biased, even with a large sample. Before using a figure, ask: who didn't reply, and why?

Data-entry errors

Last trap, and a more mundane one: errors.

Three common errors

Three errors come up often. An impossible value, like an age of two hundred and thirty. Mixed units: a salary of two thousand five hundred euros entered as two point five, in thousands, in a column in euros. And duplicates, the same person counted twice. A single outlandish value can be enough to skew an average.

  • Impossible value: an age of 230
  • Mixed units: €2,500 entered as "2.5"
  • Duplicate: the same person counted twice

Exercise

An unemployment rate goes from 7.1% to 8.4% the year the questionnaire changes. What should you check?

Your turn. A fictional unemployment rate rises from seven point one to eight point four percent, in the very year the statistics office changes its questionnaire. What should you check before concluding that unemployment is rising?

Solution

First, read the methodology note: did the definition, or the way the questions are asked, change? Then look for a recalculated series, where past years are estimated with the new method. Only compare figures defined and measured the same way.

  • Read the methodology note: did the definition change?
  • Look for a series recalculated with the new method
Answer : Same definition, same method

Key takeaways

Key takeaways. Faced with a sudden jump, look for a change of definition or method. Faced with missing answers, ask who didn't reply, and why. And faced with a strange value, check the data entry before interpreting it.

  • A sudden jump? Look for a change of method
  • Missing answers? Ask who didn't reply
  • A strange value? Check the data entry
