S3-06

Does ice cream make people drown?

When ice cream sales go up, so do drownings. Should we ban ice cream? Of course not: a third variable, hot weather, drives both. Learn to spot three explanations that have nothing to do with cause and effect (confounding, reverse causation, coincidence), and what it actually takes to establish a cause. Reasoning examples, no real data. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

Learning objectives

  • Suggest a confounding variable for a given correlation

Prerequisites

When ice cream sales go up, so do drownings

When ice cream sales go up, drownings go up too. Does that mean ice cream makes people drown?

CHALLENGE

Ice cream and drownings rise together. Why?

Take a few seconds. How would you explain these two things rising together?

The third factor

The answer comes down to two words: hot weather.

Hot weather pulls both strings

Hot weather affects both. When it's hot, people buy more ice cream. When it's hot, people also swim more, and drownings go up. So the two curves rise together, even though neither one causes the other.

  • It's hot → people buy more ice cream
  • It's hot → more swimming → more drownings
  • Ice cream and drownings rise together, with no causal link

Solution

Here's the answer to the challenge: ice cream and drownings share a common cause. Hot weather is what's called a confounding variable.

  • Ice cream and drownings share a common cause
Answer : Hot weather: a confounding variable

Correlation: two variables move together. Causation: one makes the other move.

Let's be clear about the difference. A correlation only says that two variables move together. Causation says that if you change one, the other changes. You can read the first straight from data. The second takes much more.

Three explanations without a cause

When two variables are correlated, there are at least three explanations that involve no cause and effect.

1. Confounding

First, confounding: a third factor affects both variables. For example, the more firefighters there are at a fire, the more damage there is. Firefighters don't cause the damage. A big fire brings lots of firefighters, and does lots of damage.

  • A third factor affects both
  • The more firefighters at a fire, the more damage
  • Hidden factor: the size of the fire

2. Reverse causation

Second, reverse causation: there is a cause, but it runs the other way. People who see a doctor often are ill more often. Doctors aren't making them ill. Being ill is what brings people to the doctor.

  • The cause runs the other way
  • People who see a doctor often are more often ill
  • Illness is what brings people to the doctor

3. Coincidence

Third, coincidence. Two series that both rise over the years will be correlated, even if they have nothing to do with each other: the passing of time is enough. And if you compare hundreds of series, some will end up looking alike purely by chance.

  • Two series that both rise over time
  • Compare lots of series: some will match by chance

Strong doesn't mean causal

One last trap, and a very common one.

A very strong correlation can be entirely due to confounding

People often assume a very strong correlation must be causal. It isn't so. Ice cream and drownings can be very strongly correlated, and hot weather can still explain the whole link. The strength of a correlation tells you how closely two variables move together, never why.

How do you establish a cause?

So how do you establish a cause? In a randomized experiment, participants are assigned to groups by chance: this tends to balance both known and unknown factors across the groups, on average over repeated random assignments. But in any one experiment, some differences may remain simply by chance; randomization therefore strengthens a causal interpretation, but it does not guarantee perfectly identical groups. When an experiment isn't possible, you at least need to name the possible confounders and account for them; econometrics devotes a whole pillar to this question.

  • Random assignment tends to balance factors, on average over assignments
  • In any one experiment, differences can remain by chance
  • No experiment? Name the confounders, and measure them

Exercise

Find a non-causal explanation for each: 1. Kids with bigger feet read better. 2. Students who get private tutoring have lower grades. 3. Smartphone sales and rents rose together for ten years.

Your turn. For each of these three correlations, find an explanation that isn't causal. One: children with bigger feet read better. Two: students who get private tutoring have lower grades. Three: smartphone sales and rents rose together for ten years.

Solution

Here's one possible answer for each. One, confounding: age makes feet grow and reading improve at the same time. Two, reverse causation: it's often low grades that lead students to get tutoring. Three, coincidence: two series that rise over time, with no link between them.

  • 1. Confounding: age grows feet and improves reading
  • 2. Reverse causation: low grades lead to tutoring
  • 3. Coincidence: two series that rise over time
Answer : Confounding, reverse causation, coincidence

Key takeaways

Let's recap. One: a correlation only says that two variables move together. Two: before you conclude there's a cause, look for confounding, reverse causation or coincidence. Three: a strong correlation is no more causal than a weak one.

  • Correlation: two variables move together
  • Before concluding: confounding, reverse causation, coincidence?
  • A strong correlation is no more causal
