S3-09

A dataset from start to finish: the full workflow

A table of numbers lands on your desk. What do you do? You do not start by computing. With a fictional case study — monthly births in an imaginary region — we walk through the full workflow: ask a question, check the provenance, clean, describe, chart, state the limits, and conclude in three honest sentences. Fictional data. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

A table of numbers lands on your desk. What do you do?

A colleague sends you a table of data, twelve rows, two columns. You need to 'get something out of it' by tomorrow. Where do you start?

Monthly births, fictional region

Twelve months, January to December, and a number of births for each. You are tempted to press 'compute the mean' straight away. We'll resist, and go through seven steps, in order.

Step 1: which question?

Before any number, a question.

The question comes first

What do we want to know? Here, three questions in one: are there more births in summer than in winter, by how much, and what could that mean? Without a question, the data speak for themselves — and that is never good.

  • Are there more births in summer than in winter?
  • Is the gap large or marginal?
  • What might it hide?

Step 2: provenance

Where do these numbers come from?

Five questions to ask about the source

Who produced the number: an official body, a survey, an estimate? What exactly: a declared birth, a hospital birth, a birth before a certain date? Which population: the whole region, or one city? Which period: a single year, or an average over ten years? And when: numbers from 2015 do not say the same as those from 2024. In our example, we leave this blank on purpose, to see what the workflow reveals.

  • Who produced the number?
  • What exactly: the definition
  • On which population?
  • Over which period?
  • When were these numbers dated?

Step 3: the data speak before the mean

Before summarising, look.

The 12 months, as bars

Draw the chart before the mean. And you see the shape straight away: an early-year dip, a mid-year peak, and intermediate months that rise and fall. The mean alone would have hidden this shape. Whether it reflects a true seasonality will be judged on several years, not on one.

Step 4: describe with a few numbers

Now the numbers, but only those that add something.

Four useful numbers

Monthly mean: eight hundred and ninety. Lowest month: February, seven hundred and ninety. Highest: July, one thousand and ten. The gap is two hundred and twenty, about twenty-eight percent of the minimum. This last number is the most informative: it quantifies the summer peak.

  • Monthly mean: 890
  • Lowest month: February (790)
  • Highest month: July (1,010)
  • Gap between the two: 220, about 28%

Step 5: smooth to see the trend

3-month moving average

The smoothed series confirms the shape: it climbs steadily from the start of the year through June, peaks around July, and falls back through December. The monthly waves vanish, the trend appears.

Step 6: the limits

Then say what these numbers do not say.

What the table does not tell you

With no source or date, you cannot check. A single year does not tell you if this seasonality repeats. There is no breakdown by area or by age, so the gap could come from one sub-population only. And there is no standard deviation: the one thousand and ten in July could be the mean of very dispersed years.

  • No source or date: you cannot check
  • One year only: no long trend
  • No breakdown: urban vs rural, mother's age
  • No standard deviation: 1,010 may be the mean of very different years

Step 7: conclude in three sentences

Finish with three honest sentences.

The conclusion fits in three sentences

One, what we observe. In this fictional region, July records about twenty-eight percent more births than February, in the data we received. Two, what it could be. We cannot say, on one year, whether this is a seasonality that repeats or a one-off variation specific to that year. Three, what we would need. Several years of data to test repetition, and a breakdown by area or by age to know what drives it.

  • What we observe: the summer peak is about 28% above the winter trough
  • What it could be: a cycle, a one-off, or both — indistinguishable on one year
  • What we would need: several years, and a breakdown

Exercise

A table of data you have never seen lands on your desk. Write three questions to ask before any chart.

A table arrives, you have never seen it. Before any chart, before any mean, write three questions. Pause.

Solution

One possible answer. Who produced the number, what exactly, when: the five questions of provenance. One period or several: no trend without several points. Is the definition the same everywhere: a change of definition can create a false jump. With these three questions, you are already halfway there.

  • Who produced the number, what exactly, when?
  • Is there one period or several?
  • Is the definition the same everywhere?
Answer : Provenance, period, consistent definition

Key points

Key points. A question comes before any calculation. The workflow is linear: provenance, cleaning, description, chart, limits, conclusion. And three honest sentences, with their limits, beat a table of numbers to two decimal places.

  • Question before calculation
  • Provenance, cleaning, description, chart, limits, conclusion
  • Three honest sentences beat a table of precise numbers
