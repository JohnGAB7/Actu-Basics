S4-02

Simulating chance: the frequency settles

Toss a coin ten times, then ten thousand: what happens? Chance is unpredictable one toss at a time, but very regular in large numbers. With an imaginary simulation, we watch the frequency of heads close in on fifty percent. And we learn to estimate a probability when the exact calculation would be painful: at least four heads in a row in twenty tosses — frequent or rare? Worked exercise at the end. Fictional data. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

Learning objectives

  • Describe how the observed frequency stabilises as n grows
  • Use a simulation to estimate a probability

Prerequisites

  • S4.01: what is a probability

10 tosses, then 10,000: what do we see?

A fair coin. We toss it ten times, then one hundred, then ten thousand. What changes?

Ten tosses: the unpredictable

Start with ten tosses.

An imaginary 10-toss series

Ten tosses, six heads, four tails. Final frequency: sixty percent. Is this 'abnormal'? No: in ten tosses, getting six heads or six tails happens very often, by pure chance.

  • 2 · Tails · 1 · 50%

  • 5 · Tails · 3 · 60%

  • 7 · Heads · 4 · 57%

  • 10 · Heads · 6 · 60%

Many more tosses: stabilisation

Now, the same set-up, repeated ten thousand times.

Frequency of heads over n tosses (imaginary simulation)

At ten tosses, the frequency can hit sixty percent. At one hundred, it already hovers around fifty. At ten thousand, it is very close to fifty percent. This is the law of large numbers: the observed frequency stabilises around the probability as the number of trials grows.

A classic mistake: 'catching up'

Beware a very common mistake.

The coin has no memory

People often say: after eight heads, tails is due. Not true. The coin has no memory. The law of large numbers does not catch up with gaps: it dilutes them, because new tosses weigh more and more in the average.

  • If you saw 8 heads in a row
  • The next is not more likely to be tails
  • It stays at 50%
  • The law of large numbers dilutes gaps; it does not catch them up

Estimating a probability by simulation

A simulation estimates a probability when the exact calculation would be painful.

The recipe for a simulation

Reproduce the experiment — twenty tosses of a coin — one thousand times. For each, record yes or no. Count the frequency of 'yes'. That estimates the probability. The more you repeat, the sharper the estimate.

  • Reproduce the experiment once (20 tosses)
  • Note whether the event happened (at least 4 heads in a row)
  • Repeat 1,000 times
  • The frequency estimates the probability

Your turn

Your turn.

Exercise

In 20 tosses of a fair coin, is a run of at least 4 heads in a row surprising?

Twenty tosses, a fair coin. At least four heads in a row, somewhere in the series. Do you think this is rare, or common? Give a range. Pause.

Solution

Do not confuse two things. The simulation produces a frequency: if you redo the experiment of one thousand series, you often land around forty-eight percent, with a variability of about plus or minus three points between simulations. The exact probability, on the other hand, is computed: through a recurrence that counts the two to the twenty binary sequences with no run of four. Result: forty-seven point eighty percent exactly. The simulation approximates this probability; it does not give it. Either way, the conclusion is the same: a run of four heads happens almost one time in two.

  • Simulation (1,000 series): frequency around 48%, varying by about ± 3 points between runs
  • Exact calculation (recurrence over 2²⁰ sequences): 47.80%
  • The simulation approximates the probability; it does not equal it
Answer : Simulation ≈ 48%, exact probability = 47.80%

Key points

Key points. Law of large numbers: the observed frequency stabilises around the probability as the number of trials grows. Chance has no memory: it catches nothing up. And a simulation, done well and repeated, often beats an exact calculation.

  • Law of large numbers: the frequency stabilises as n grows
  • The coin has no memory: no catch-up
  • Simulating estimates a probability without a calculation
