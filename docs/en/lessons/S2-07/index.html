S2-07

Same average, opposite temperatures: measuring spread

Two towns have the same average temperature over the year: 12 °C. Yet one has mild winters and cool summers, while the other has a colder winter and a warmer summer. We learn to measure that difference with two simple tools, the range and the interquartile range, and when to use each. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional towns and temperatures, checked calculations. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

Videos · Video in preparation

Learning objectives

  • Compute the range and the interquartile range
  • Explain why a centre alone is not enough to describe a distribution
  • Know why the interquartile range copes better with extreme values than the range

Prerequisites

  • S2.06: quantiles, quartiles and box plots

Two towns, 12 °C on average: same temperatures?

Two towns have exactly the same average temperature over the year: twelve degrees. Do they really have the same temperatures from month to month?

Same centre, two stories

Let's look at all twelve months, not just the average.

Average temperature of each month (°C)

Here are the average temperatures of each month in two imaginary towns. Town A, by the sea, goes from seven degrees in January to seventeen in summer. Town B, deep inside a continent, goes from one degree in January to twenty-three in July. And yet, over the year, both average twelve degrees.

  • Town A: from 7 °C in January to 17 °C in summer
  • Town B: from 1 °C in January to 23 °C in July
  • Average over the year: 12 °C for both

A: 7, 8, 9, 11, 13, 15, 17, 17, 15, 13, 10, 9. B: 1, 3, 7, 12, 16, 20, 23, 22, 18, 13, 7, 2.

The centre says where you are. The spread says how far you can stray

The average tells you where the centre is. It doesn't tell you how far you can stray from it. For that, you need to measure the spread.

The range

First tool, the simplest one: the range.

Range = maximum − minimum

The range is the gap between the largest and the smallest value. For town A, seventeen minus seven: ten degrees. For town B, twenty-three minus one: twenty-two degrees, more than double.

\text{A}: 17-7=10\ \text{°C}

One extreme value is enough to change it

Its weakness: it depends on only two values, the two extremes. A single extreme value, such as an exceptionally cold month, can change the range a lot.

The interquartile range

Second tool: we look only at the middle half.

The middle half: 6 °C versus 14 °C

Let's sort the twelve months of each town and draw their boxes. For town A, the middle half runs from nine to fifteen degrees: an interquartile range of six. For town B, it runs from five to nineteen: fourteen degrees. The medians are almost the same; the boxes are not at all.

[Chart for this step]

Quartiles of the 12 months. A: Q1 = 9, Q3 = 15. B: Q1 = 5, Q3 = 19 (halfway between the 3rd and 4th values of each half).

Range or interquartile range?

So which one should you use? The range is quick, but a single extreme moves it. The interquartile range is more robust: it ignores the top quarter and the bottom quarter. Either way, always give a centre and a spread, never a centre alone.

  • Range: quick, but sensitive to extremes
  • Interquartile range: robust, it ignores the top and bottom quarters
  • Always: a centre and a spread

Exercise

X: 10, 11, 11, 11, 12. Y: 7, 9, 11, 13, 15. Z: 1, 6, 11, 16, 21. Rank them from least to most spread out, then check with the range.

Your turn. Three data sets all have a mean of eleven. X: ten, eleven, eleven, eleven, twelve. Y: seven, nine, eleven, thirteen, fifteen. Z: one, six, eleven, sixteen, twenty-one. Rank them from least to most spread out at a glance, then check with the range.

Solution

Let's check. The range of X is twelve minus ten: two. For Y, fifteen minus seven: eight. For Z, twenty-one minus one: twenty. Same centre, and yet X is the tightest, then Y, then Z.

  • X: 12 − 10 = 2
  • Y: 15 − 7 = 8
  • Z: 21 − 1 = 20
Answer : X, then Y, then Z

Key points

Key points. The same mean doesn't mean the same distribution. The range, maximum minus minimum, is sensitive to extremes. The interquartile range, Q3 minus Q1, is more robust.

  • Same mean doesn't mean same distribution
  • Range = max − min, sensitive to extremes
  • Interquartile range = Q3 − Q1, more robust
