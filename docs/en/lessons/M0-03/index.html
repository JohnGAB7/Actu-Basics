What does a point on a chart really tell you?

A point on a chart carries two pieces of information at once: one on the horizontal axis, one on the vertical axis. We learn to check what each axis measures and in which unit, to read a point's coordinates and to work out the step of a scale. Fictional data. A point on a chart: what does it say, exactly?

A point on a chart. What does it say, exactly? Before reading a trend, you need to be able to read a single point.

Two axes, two questions

It all starts with the two axes.

Before looking at the points

Before looking at the points, ask three questions. What does the horizontal axis measure? Here, time, in weeks. What does the vertical axis measure? Here, the height of a plant. And in which unit? Centimetres.

  • Horizontal axis: what does it measure? Here, weeks
  • Vertical axis: what does it measure? Here, height
  • In which unit? Here, centimetres

Reading a point

Now let's read a point.

Height of a plant, week by week

Each point is one measurement. Take this one. Go straight down to the horizontal axis: week three. Go left to the vertical axis: nine centimetres. So this point says: in week three, the plant was nine centimetres tall.

[Chart for this step]

Fictional data. Height in centimetres.

A point = two numbers, in order

We write these two numbers in brackets, always in the same order: horizontal axis first, then vertical axis. Three, nine. Swap them, and you'd be talking about week nine, which isn't on this chart.

(x,\ y)=(3,\ 9)

Reading a scale

One last skill: reading a scale when not every mark is labelled.

The step of a regular scale

On an axis, only zero and twenty are labelled, with four equal intervals between them. Each mark is worth twenty minus zero, divided by four: five centimetres. Count the intervals between the marks, not the number of tick marks.

\mathrm{step}=\dfrac{\mathrm{top\ value}-\mathrm{bottom\ value}}{\mathrm{number\ of\ intervals}}

Also check where the axis starts

One last habit: check where the vertical axis starts. If it doesn't start at zero, small gaps can look huge. We'll come back to this in the lesson on misleading charts.

Exercise

An axis runs from 0 to 50, with 10 intervals. What is one mark worth?

Your turn. An axis runs from zero to fifty, with ten equal intervals. What is one mark worth?

Solution

The total gap is fifty minus zero, fifty. Divide it by ten intervals: each mark is worth five.

  • Total gap: 50 − 0 = 50
  • Divided by 10 intervals
Answer : 5 per interval

Three questions before reading a chart

Before reading a chart, three questions. What does each axis measure? In which unit? And what is the step of the scale, and where does the axis start?
