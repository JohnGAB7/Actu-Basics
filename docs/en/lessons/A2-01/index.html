A2-01

Why is the average claim cost more predictable with 10,000 policies?

Insuring one phone is a gamble: the insurer pays either nothing or $500. Insuring 10,000 phones is almost predictable. Why? In this episode of our actuarial science series, we treat the cost of a policy as a random variable, compute its expected value and standard deviation, and see how pooling shrinks the uncertainty per policy: sigma divided by the square root of n. We also see what pooling cannot do: it makes losses more predictable, not automatically profitable. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional insurer and figures, checked calculations.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the cost of one policy as a random variable, with its expected value and standard deviation
  • Explain why the average cost per policy becomes more predictable as the number of policies grows
  • Use the rule: standard deviation of the average = sigma divided by the square root of n
  • State the conditions pooling relies on: independence, similar risks, enough policies
  • Tell apart the pure premium, the commercial premium and the technical result

Prerequisites

  • A0.01: the actuarial profession (pure premium)
  • Standard deviation, intuitively (statistics series: variance and standard deviation)

One phone is a gamble. 10,000 phones are almost predictable.

For one phone, the outcome is highly uncertain: the insurer pays either nothing or five hundred dollars. Insure ten thousand phones, and you can predict the cost per phone remarkably well. Why? That is the idea behind all insurance: pooling.

Back to JG's Holding and its phones

In the first episode, our fictional insurer, JG's Holding, insured phones against theft. Each phone had a two percent chance of being stolen in a year, and a theft cost five hundred dollars. We found a pure premium of ten dollars per phone. Today, we ask a deeper question: how reliable is that ten dollars?

One policy: a gamble

Let's look at a single phone first.

One phone, one year: what JG's Holding pays

For a single phone, only two things can happen. Ninety-eight percent of the time, there is no theft, and JG's Holding pays nothing. Two percent of the time, the phone is stolen, and JG's Holding pays five hundred dollars. The expected cost weighs each outcome by its probability, which gives ten dollars. But notice: JG's Holding never actually pays ten dollars for this phone; it pays either zero or five hundred.

  • 98% of the time: $0
  • 2% of the time: $500
  • Expected cost: 0.98 × $0 + 0.02 × $500 = $10
  • But JG's Holding never actually pays $10

The expected value is a long-run average, not a forecast

The cost of one policy is what statisticians call a random variable: a number whose value depends on chance. Its expected value, ten dollars here, is the average cost you would get over a very large number of identical phones. For any single phone, it is not a forecast of what will happen.

How uncertain is one phone?

To measure that uncertainty, we use the standard deviation, the typical distance from the average. First the variance: the expected square of the cost, zero point zero two times five hundred squared, minus the square of the mean, ten squared. That gives four thousand nine hundred. Its square root, the standard deviation, is seventy dollars.

\[\mathrm{Var}(X)=0.02\times 500^2-10^2=4900\]

A spread of $70 around an average of $10

So for one phone, the uncertainty is seven times larger than the average itself. Setting a price for a single phone, on its own, is close to a bet.

Many policies: pooling

Now let's insure many phones at once.

How many thefts in a year?

Let's count the thefts. With one hundred phones, JG's Holding expects about two thefts, give or take one point four. With ten thousand phones, it expects about two hundred, give or take fourteen. The spread grows, but much more slowly than the number of phones: it is seventy percent of the average with one hundred phones, and only seven percent with ten thousand.

  • 100 phones: about 2 thefts, give or take 1.4
  • 10,000 phones: about 200 thefts, give or take 14
  • Relative spread: 1.4 ÷ 2 = 70%, but 14 ÷ 200 = 7%

Independent thefts: the number of thefts follows a binomial distribution. Standard deviation: √(n × 0.02 × 0.98).

Uncertainty of the average cost per phone

Now look at the average cost per phone. When the policy costs are independent and identically distributed, as here with the same two percent and the same five hundred dollars, the standard deviation of their average is sigma divided by the square root of n, the number of phones. With one hundred phones, seventy divided by ten gives seven dollars. With ten thousand phones, seventy divided by one hundred gives just seventy cents.

\[\sigma_{\bar{X}}=\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}=\dfrac{70}{\sqrt{n}}\]

Standard deviation of the cost per phone

Here is the same idea in a picture. The average cost per phone is ten dollars in all three cases. But the uncertainty around it falls from seventy dollars for one phone, to seven dollars for one hundred phones, to seventy cents for ten thousand. With many independent policies, the random ups and downs largely cancel out.

Average cost per phone: $10 in all three cases. Independent thefts.

The law of large numbers: the average cost tends towards $10

This is the law of large numbers at work. As the number of independent, similar policies grows, the average cost per policy tends to converge towards its expected value, ten dollars. That is what pooling means: sharing risks among many people turns an unpredictable cost into a fairly predictable one.

What pooling cannot do

Pooling is powerful, but it has limits.

Premium $11 per phone: chance that thefts cost more than the premiums

Suppose JG's Holding charges eleven dollars per phone: the ten dollar pure premium plus a one dollar safety margin. With one hundred phones, it collects one thousand one hundred dollars, so three thefts are enough to cost more than that: this happens about thirty-two percent of the time. With ten thousand phones, it takes two hundred and twenty-one thefts or more, which happens about seven point three percent of the time. Pooling makes a bad year much less likely, but never impossible.

  • 100 phones: 3 thefts or more, about 32%
  • 10,000 phones: 221 thefts or more, about 7.3%
  • Much smaller, but never zero

$11 = pure premium $10 + a $1 safety margin; expenses ignored. Exact binomial probabilities.

Pooling works only if…

Pooling relies on conditions that actuaries always check. The risks must be largely independent: a hailstorm that damages every car in a town at once does not cancel out. The risks must be similar enough to be grouped together. There must be enough policies for the averages to settle. And the probability must stay stable: if thefts suddenly become more common, last year's average no longer applies.

  • Risks are independent
  • Risks are similar
  • There are enough policies
  • The probability stays stable

Predictable is not the same as profitable

Finally, let's be precise about what pooling does not promise. The pure premium is only the expected cost of claims per policy. The commercial premium, the price actually charged, adds loadings for expenses, commissions, uncertainty and capital. And the technical result, which in this simplified example is premiums earned minus claims incurred and operating expenses, can still be negative if the price is too low, the expenses too high, or a rare extreme event strikes.

  • Pure premium: the expected claims per policy
  • Commercial premium: pure premium plus loadings
  • Technical result (simplified): premiums earned minus claims incurred and expenses

Exercise

JG's Holding insures 2,500 phones. Expected thefts? Standard deviation of the average cost per phone?

Your turn. JG's Holding now insures two thousand five hundred phones, with the same two percent chance of theft and the same five hundred dollar cost. How many thefts should it expect? And what is the standard deviation of the average cost per phone?

Solution

Let's work it out. The expected number of thefts is two thousand five hundred times two percent, that is fifty. For one phone, the standard deviation is still seventy dollars. For the average of two thousand five hundred phones, we divide seventy by the square root of two thousand five hundred, which is fifty. So JG's Holding expects fifty thefts, and the standard deviation of the average cost per phone is about one dollar forty.

  • Expected thefts: 2,500 × 2% = 50
  • One phone: σ = $70
  • Average of 2,500 phones: 70 ÷ √2,500 = 70 ÷ 50
Answer : 50 thefts; standard deviation ≈ $1.40 per phone

Key takeaways

Key takeaways. For one policy, the cost is either zero or five hundred dollars, with an expected value of ten. For the average of n independent, identically distributed policies, the spread shrinks to sigma divided by the square root of n. Pooling only works with independent, similar risks and enough policies. And a predictable cost is not automatically a profit.

  • One policy: a cost of $0 or $500, expected value $10
  • Average of n independent, identical policies: spread σ ÷ √n
  • Pooling needs independence, similar risks, enough policies
  • Predictable is not profitable
