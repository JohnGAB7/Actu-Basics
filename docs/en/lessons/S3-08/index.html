S3-08

Indices and moving averages: reading change over time

How can we compare the change in bread prices and rent since 2000? With two imaginary series, we meet the base-100 index: bring everyone back to the same starting point, then compare paths. Then, with another series that fluctuates from month to month, we smooth with a moving average to reveal the trend. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional data. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

⏱ 3 min read✎ Written by JG↻ Updated 2026-11-13
Videos · Video in preparation

Learning objectives

  • Compute a base-100 index to compare two series
  • Smooth a series with a moving average
  • Tell the short-term fluctuations of a series apart from its trend

Prerequisites

  • S1.08: percentages and points
  • S2.04: what the mean really says

Since 2000, has bread or rent gone up more?

Imagine two prices tracked since the year 2000: bread and rent. How do we tell which one has gone up more?

Two series at very different levels

The problem is that the two series do not live in the same world.

Bread and rent, 7 imaginary years

Seven years. Bread goes from two dollars fifty to three dollars fifty; rent, from five hundred to eight hundred and fifty dollars. Putting both columns on the same chart is impossible: the orders of magnitude are too different.

  • 2000 · 2.50 · 500
  • 2001 · 2.60 · 560
  • 2002 · 2.80 · 620
  • 2003 · 3.00 · 680
  • 2004 · 3.20 · 730
  • 2005 · 3.30 · 800
  • 2006 · 3.50 · 850

The base-100 index: back to the same start

The trick is to bring both series back to the same starting point.

index = value / base value × 100

The index at time t is the value at time t divided by the base value, times one hundred. We pick a reference year — here 2000 — and give it the value one hundred.

\[index_t = (value_t / value_base) × 100\]

The same series, in base 100

There we are. In 2000, both series are at one hundred. In 2006, bread is at one hundred and forty — up forty percent — and rent at one hundred and seventy — up seventy percent. Rent has clearly gone up more, in our imaginary example.

  • 2000 · 100 · 100
  • 2001 · 104 · 112
  • 2002 · 112 · 124
  • 2003 · 120 · 136
  • 2004 · 128 · 146
  • 2005 · 132 · 160
  • 2006 · 140 · 170

A wavy series

Now, a new example.

Loaves sold per month, one imaginary bakery

Nine months of sales from an imaginary bakery. The series goes up overall, but fluctuates from month to month. Nine months are too few to claim a seasonality: you would need several years to see whether the pattern repeats. What we can do is reveal the trend by smoothing the fluctuations.

Imaginary bakery: numbers made up.

The moving average: smoothing the series

A moving average replaces each month with the average of its neighbours.

Moving average over 3 points

Take three consecutive values, average them, and place the average at the centre. Then move by one step, and repeat. For our nine months, we get seven smoothed values.

  • Take three consecutive values
  • Average them
  • Place the average at the centre of the three
  • Move by one step, and repeat

Raw series and 3-month moving average

The first and last months have no average: you need neighbours. For the rest, the smoothed series climbs steadily: from eleven to nineteen. The monthly fluctuations are dampened, the trend comes through.

  • 1 · 10 · —
  • 2 · 12 · 11.00
  • 3 · 11 · 12.33
  • 4 · 14 · 13.67
  • 5 · 16 · 15.00
  • 6 · 15 · 16.33
  • 7 · 18 · 17.67
  • 8 · 20 · 19.00
  • 9 · 19 · —

Two classic mistakes

Two traps to avoid.

The two traps

One: comparing a price in dollars to another price in dollars tells you nothing if the levels are very different. Use a base-100 index, which brings everything to the same scale. Two: mistaking a short-term fluctuation for a trend, or vice versa. The moving average separates the two — provided you have enough data. Seasonality is only confirmed after several complete cycles.

  • Comparing levels instead of changes
  • Mistaking a fluctuation for a trend (and vice-versa)

Exercise

Series 50, 55, 60. Convert to a base-100 index on the first value.

Fifty, fifty-five, sixty. Convert this series to a base-100 index, using the first value as the base. Pause.

Solution

The base, fifty, takes the value one hundred. Fifty-five divided by fifty times one hundred: one hundred and ten. Sixty divided by fifty times one hundred: one hundred and twenty. The indexed series is therefore one hundred, one hundred and ten, one hundred and twenty.

  • Base: 50 → 100
  • 55 / 50 × 100 = 110
  • 60 / 50 × 100 = 120
Answer : Indices: 100, 110, 120

Key points

Key points. The base-100 index brings all series to the same starting point and lets you compare their paths. A moving average replaces each value with the average of its neighbours, and lets the trend show by dampening the fluctuations. And always compare changes, not levels.

  • Base-100 index: bring everyone to the same starting point
  • Moving average: smooth to see the trend
  • Compare changes, not levels
