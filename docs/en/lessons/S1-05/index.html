S1-05

2.4 million answers, and wrong: selection bias

In 1936, the American magazine Literary Digest mailed out about 10 million ballots to predict the presidential election. Nearly 2.4 million came back: the poll said Alf Landon would win with 54%. Franklin Roosevelt was re-elected with 61% of the vote. How could such a huge sample get it so wrong? We learn what a representative sample is, and why size doesn't fix selection bias. Sources: D. Lusinchi, Social Science History, 2012; S. Lohr and J. M. Brick, Statistics, Politics and Policy, 2017. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

The giant poll of 1936

We're in the United States, in nineteen thirty-six.

The Literary Digest predicts the election

The Literary Digest magazine wants to predict the presidential election. It mails out about ten million ballots. Nearly two point four million come back. The magazine announces that the Republican, Alf Landon, will win with fifty-four percent of the vote, against forty-one percent for President Franklin Roosevelt. On election day, Roosevelt is re-elected, with sixty-one percent of the vote.

  • About 10 million ballots mailed out
  • Nearly 2.4 million come back
  • Published forecast: Landon 54%, Roosevelt 41%
  • Result: Roosevelt re-elected with 61% of the vote

Figures as reported by D. Lusinchi, Social Science History, 2012.

One detail matters a lot: only twenty-four percent of the ballots came back.

A representative sample

To understand the mistake, we need to define what a good sample is.

Three words to know

The population, here, is all the voters. The sample is the people you actually ask. A sample is representative when it looks like the population for what you want to measure: here, the vote.

  • Population: all the voters
  • Sample: the people you ask
  • Representative: it looks like the population

Two filters between the voters and the answers

Between the voters and the ballots received, there were two filters. The first was the mailing list: the addresses came notably from telephone directories and lists of car owners, which didn't represent all voters. The second was who answered: the people who bothered to mail back their ballot leaned more towards Landon. Researchers still debate how much each filter mattered, but both helped make the sample unrepresentative.

  • The list: addresses taken notably from telephone directories and car owner lists
  • The answers: those who mail back the ballot don't vote like the others

According to Squire (1988), cited by Lohr and Brick (2017), both biases played a part.

That's selection bias: the sample no longer looks like the population

This is called selection bias: the way the sample is chosen or collected makes it systematically different from the population.

Size doesn't fix bias

And this is where the lesson gets surprising.

More answers, so what?

In a randomly drawn sample, asking more people reduces the uncertainty due to chance. But it doesn't fix selection bias. With two point four million answers, the Literary Digest measured very precisely the opinion of the people who answered it, not that of all voters.

  • More answers reduce chance error
  • But not bias: you measure the wrong population very precisely

A big sample badly chosen is still badly chosen

Remember this: a big sample that's badly chosen is still badly chosen. What matters first is how you choose it, not how big it is.

Exercise

Who's missing? Reviews left online. A poll by landline only. A survey on exercise, handed out outside a gym.

Your turn. In each of these three surveys, who is likely to be missing? Reviews of a restaurant left online. A poll carried out by landline only. A survey on physical activity, handed out outside a gym.

Solution

Online, the people who leave a review aren't necessarily like all customers: very good or very bad experiences are often over-represented. By landline, you miss everyone who doesn't have one, often younger people. Outside a gym, you miss precisely the people who don't go. In all three cases, the sample doesn't look like the population.

  • Online: the very happy and very unhappy write more
  • Landline: missing those who don't have one
  • Gym: missing those who don't go
Answer : Three samples that select themselves

What to remember

Let's recap. When you see a poll, ask how the sample was chosen. Ask who didn't answer. And remember that size is never a substitute for representativeness.

  • Ask: how was the sample chosen?
  • Ask: who didn't answer?
  • Size is no substitute for representativeness
