Same number, disaster or detail? Reading a chart

The same price index can look like it is soaring or flat: it all depends on the axis. We learn to spot an axis that doesn't start at zero, a dual scale and a misleading area, and to check the title, unit, period and source before drawing conclusions. Fictional examples, checked calculations. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

⏱ 3 min read✎ Written by JG↻ Updated 2026-11-08
Videos · Video in preparation

Learning objectives

  • Spot a truncated axis, a dual scale and a misleading area
  • Check the title, unit, period and source before interpreting a chart

Prerequisites

  • S1.08: percentages and points
  • M0.03: reading a chart

The same number: disaster or detail?

The same number can look like a disaster, or like a detail. All it takes is a different chart.

The axis that doesn't start at zero

The first trap, and the most common: the vertical axis.

Price of a basket, axis from 97 to 101

Here is a fictional index of the price of a shopping basket. The axis runs from ninety-seven to one hundred and one. The line seems to shoot up: you might think prices are exploding.

Fictional index: 98 in 2021, 101 in 2024.

The same numbers, axis from 0

These are exactly the same numbers. But this time, the axis starts at zero. The line is almost flat.

What really changed

What is the real rise? One hundred and one minus ninety-eight, divided by ninety-eight: about three point one percent over three years. The rise is real, but far less dramatic than the first chart suggests.

\[\dfrac{101-98}{98}\approx 3.1\%\]

The slope depends on the scale: read the axes first

Remember this: the slope of a line depends on the scale. An axis that doesn't start at zero isn't forbidden, it shows small changes. But you have to notice it before you judge.

Two more traps

Two more traps come up often.

Dual y-axes: the two lines cross

First trap, dual y-axes: two lines, each with its own vertical axis. On the left, fictional bike rentals; on the right, scooter rentals. The lines cross in twenty twenty-two: you would think something happened that year. This visual crossing doesn't mean the two quantities are equal, or that they move the same way: it depends on the scales chosen.

Fictional bike rentals (left axis) and scooter rentals (right axis).

Same numbers, right axis changed

These are the same numbers. We only changed the right axis: scooters now seem to stall at the bottom. By adjusting the two axes, you can greatly change where the lines appear. In fact, bikes grew by forty percent, and scooters by one hundred and twenty percent.

Area: twice as tall and twice as wide

Second trap, area. We show a number as a square of side one. If we double the value, but double both the height and the width of the square, it holds four small squares. That's misleading: when area stands for the value, it should only double, not be multiplied by four.

  • One number: a square of side 1
  • Double the number: a square of side 2
  • The area is multiplied by 4

Read a chart like a text

A chart is an argument. You read it like a text, before you believe it.

Four checks before interpreting

Four checks, before any interpretation. The title: what exactly is being measured? The unit: euros, percentages, an index? The period: why does the chart start at that date, and not earlier? The source: who produced the numbers?

  • Title: what exactly is measured?
  • Unit: euros, %, an index?
  • Period: why start there?
  • Source: who produced the numbers?

Exercise

A chart claims to show unemployment: axis from 7.0 to 7.4%, two months, no source. Three flaws?

Your turn. A chart claims to show how the unemployment rate has changed. The axis runs from seven to seven point four percent. Only two months are shown. And no source is given. What are the three flaws?

Solution

Here are the three flaws. The axis runs from seven to seven point four: the smallest rise looks huge. Two months is too little to establish a reliable trend. And without a source, nothing can be checked. Three flaws: a truncated axis, a period that's too short, and no source.

  • Axis from 7.0 to 7.4%: the smallest rise looks huge
  • Two months: too little for a reliable trend
  • No source: impossible to check
Answer : Truncated axis, period too short, no source

Key takeaways

Key takeaways. Read the axes before looking at the slope. Beware of areas and dual scales. And always check the title, the unit, the period and the source.

  • Read the axes before the slope
  • Beware of areas and dual scales
  • Check title, unit, period, source
