Who is this number about? Population, unit, variable

"People in France sleep 7 hours a night": who are we talking about, and what was measured? To understand a statistic, ask three questions: which population, which unit, which variable. We learn to spot the population, the unit and the variable, and we see on a small made-up village that income "per household" and income "per person" give very different figures. Learning objectives

  • Identify the population, the unit and the variable in a sentence
  • Explain why changing the unit, household or person, changes the result

Prerequisites

  • S1.01: what counts as data?

"People in France sleep 7 hours a night." Who is this about?

Imagine this headline: people in France sleep seven hours a night. Who exactly are we talking about? And what was measured?

Who, which unit, which measure

To understand a statistic, ask three questions: which population is studied, which unit is observed, and which variable is measured?

The three building blocks of a statistic

There are three building blocks. The population is the whole group being studied. The unit, also called the statistical unit, is each element of that population. The variable is what is measured on each unit.

  • Population: the whole group being studied
  • Unit: each element being counted
  • Variable: what is measured on each one

"People in France sleep 7 hours"

Let's apply them to our headline. The population: which people are we talking about? Adults, children, everyone? The unit: here, one person. The variable: sleep time. But was it reported by the people themselves, or measured by a device?

  • Population: which people? Adults? Children?
  • Unit: one person
  • Variable: sleep time, reported or measured?

A short headline often hides the population and the method

A short headline often hides the population and the method. Without them, there's no way to know whether two figures can be compared.

Change the unit, change the number

The unit you choose matters as much as the population. Let's see why.

A made-up village of 3 households

Take a made-up village of three households. Household A, one person living alone, earns two thousand euros a month. Household B, four people, earns four thousand euros. Household C, two people, earns three thousand euros.

  • Household A: 1 person, €2,000 a month
  • Household B: 4 people, €4,000
  • Household C: 2 people, €3,000

Made-up village: 3 households, 7 residents, €9,000 of income a month.

Average income in the village

What is the average income in the village? Per household, nine thousand euros divided by three households: three thousand euros. Per resident, the same nine thousand euros divided by seven residents: about one thousand two hundred and eighty-six euros.

  • Per household: 9,000 ÷ 3 = €3,000
  • Per resident: 9,000 ÷ 7 ≈ €1,286

Same village, two numbers: state the unit

It's the same village and the same money. But depending on the unit, household or person, the average income goes from three thousand to under one thousand three hundred euros. Comparing a per-household figure with a per-person figure would make no sense.

Exercise

"30% of middle-school pupils have a phone before age 11." Population? Unit? Variable?

Your turn. Here's another made-up headline: thirty percent of middle-school pupils have a phone before age eleven. What is the population, what is the unit, what is the variable?

Solution

The population is middle-school pupils, but we'd need to know which country and which year. The unit is one pupil. The variable is whether they had a phone before age eleven: a yes or no answer. In a real article, each of these three building blocks should be stated.

  • Population: middle-school pupils, but where and when?
  • Unit: one pupil
  • Variable: a phone before age 11, yes or no
Answer : Population, unit, variable: all three need to be stated

What to remember

Let's recap. Who is studied: that's the population. Which unit is observed: a person, a household or a company. And what is measured, and how: that's the variable. Before comparing two figures, check that these three building blocks are the same.

  • Who is studied? The population
  • Which unit is observed? The unit: person, household…
  • What is measured, and how? The variable
