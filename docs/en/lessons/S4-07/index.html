S4-07

Conditional probability

Does knowing it is raining change the probability of a traffic jam? With a cross-table of 100 imaginary days, we meet conditional probability: conditioning means restricting the sample space to the cases where the hypothesis holds. And we learn not to confuse P(A | B) with P(B | A), the mistake behind the prosecutor's fallacy. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional data. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

Learning objectives

  • Compute P(A | B) in a cross-table
  • Tell apart P(A | B) and P(B | A)

Prerequisites

  • S4.05: complement, union, intersection

It's raining: does the probability of a jam change?

In an imaginary city, we watch weather and traffic over one hundred days. Does knowing it is raining change the probability of a jam?

The 100 days: a cross-table

Here is the cross-table, in number of days.

The 4 cells of our 100 imaginary days

Thirty days with rain and jam. Twenty with rain and no jam. Ten with no rain but a jam. Forty with neither. Total: one hundred days.

  • Rain and jam: 30 days
  • Rain without jam: 20 days
  • No rain but jam: 10 days
  • No rain and no jam: 40 days
  • Total: 100 days

The starting probabilities

Start with the 'raw' probabilities.

The marginals of the table

Out of one hundred days, fifty days of rain — one in two. Forty days with a jam — four in ten. And on thirty days, rain and jam happen together — three in ten.

  • P(rain) = 50 / 100 = 0.50
  • P(jam) = 40 / 100 = 0.40
  • P(rain and jam) = 30 / 100 = 0.30

Conditioning: restrict the sample space

Now we learn it is raining today.

A jam, given that it's raining

Once we learn it is raining, we no longer look at all one hundred days: only the fifty rainy days. Among them, thirty have a jam. The conditional probability is thirty over fifty, or zero point sixty. Where the raw probability of a jam was zero point forty. The information 'it is raining' has raised the probability.

  • Look only at rainy days: 50 days
  • Among them, how many have a jam? 30
  • P(jam | rain) = 30 / 50 = 0.60

P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)

The general formula: P of A given B equals P of A and B divided by P of B. In our example: zero point thirty over zero point fifty, that is zero point sixty. Same result as from the table.

\[P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)\]

The other direction: P(rain | jam)

What if we flip the question?

Rain, given that there is a jam

Same table, flipped question. Look only at the forty days with a jam. Among them, thirty with rain. Conditional probability: thirty over forty, zero point seventy-five.

  • Look only at days with a jam: 40 days
  • Among them, how many with rain? 30
  • P(rain | jam) = 30 / 40 = 0.75

P(jam | rain) = 0.60, P(rain | jam) = 0.75

And here comes a surprise. P of jam given rain is zero point sixty. P of rain given jam is zero point seventy-five. Both are different, because they answer different questions. Flipping the conditioning is not neutral.

The mistake: the prosecutor's fallacy

This confusion powers a famous bad argument.

A fictional numbered case

Take a numbered example. An imaginary city of one million people. A DNA marker that, on a random person in the population, matches one time in one hundred thousand. We expect about ten random matches, plus the true culprit if they are in the city. That is eleven people with a match.

  • Imaginary city of 1,000,000 people, one true culprit among them
  • DNA marker that matches on 1 random person in 100,000
  • Expected: ≈ 10 innocents who match, plus the culprit
  • That is 11 people in total who match

The right probability

The probability that an innocent person matches is indeed one in one hundred thousand: very small. But a trial asks the reverse: what is the probability that the defendant is the culprit, given that they match? With eleven matches and only one culprit, the conditional probability is about one in eleven, or nine percent. The defendant may well be innocent. A prosecutor who presents 'one in one hundred thousand' as proof of guilt confuses the two directions.

  • P(match | innocent) = 1 / 100,000 — very small
  • P(culprit | match) ≈ 1 / 11 ≈ 9% — not at all certain
  • The two probabilities are not the same

'Most accidents happen close to home'

Same trap in the phrase: most accidents happen close to home. True, because you spend more time there. But it does not say driving close to home is more dangerous. That is P(close to home | accident), not P(accident | close to home).

Your turn

Your turn.

Exercise

From the same table, compute P(no jam | no rain) and P(no rain | no jam).

Same one hundred days. Compute the probability of no jam given no rain. Then the probability of no rain given no jam. Pause.

Solution

Fifty days without rain, forty of them without a jam: forty over fifty, zero point eighty. Sixty days without a jam, forty of them without rain: forty over sixty, about zero point sixty-seven. Two different probabilities, once again.

  • P(no jam | no rain) = 40 / 50 = 0.80
  • P(no rain | no jam) = 40 / 60 ≈ 0.67
  • Two different probabilities
Answer : 0.80 one way, 0.67 the other

Key points

Key points. Conditioning restricts the sample space to the cases where the hypothesis holds. The formula: probability of A given B equals probability of A and B divided by probability of B. And beware: flipping the conditioning changes the result, and sometimes powers a spectacular fallacy.

  • Conditioning restricts the sample space to cases where B holds
  • P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)
  • Flipping the conditioning changes the result
