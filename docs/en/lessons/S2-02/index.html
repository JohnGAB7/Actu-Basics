Why a histogram is not a bar chart

Separate bars for categories, touching bars for numbers grouped into classes. Using fictional commuting times, we see how the choice of classes changes the shape of a histogram, and why, when classes are unequal, it is the area that counts, not the height. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional examples, checked calculations. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

⏱ 3 min read✎ Written by JG↻ Updated 2026-11-08
Videos · Video in preparation

Learning objectives

  • Choose between a bar chart and a histogram according to the type of variable
  • Show that the choice of classes changes how a histogram looks
  • Correct the heights when classes have unequal widths

Prerequisites

  • S2.01: frequencies and tables
  • M0.03: reading a graph

Bars that touch, or don't: it changes everything

Two charts made of bars. On one, the bars touch; on the other, they don't. That detail changes how you read them.

The bar chart: categories

Let's start with the bar chart.

How 20 employees get to work

Here are the twenty employees from the last episode. One bar for each way of getting to work: car, public transport, bike, walking. The bars are separate, because these are distinct categories. You could put them in another order without changing the meaning.

Fictional company of 20 employees (exercise from the last episode).

The histogram: numbers in classes

Now let's take a numerical variable: commuting time, in minutes.

Commuting time, 10-minute classes

We group the commuting times into ten-minute classes. Three employees take under ten minutes, seven between ten and twenty, six between twenty and thirty, three between thirty and forty, and just one between forty and fifty. This time the bars touch: minutes run on continuously, with no gap between classes.

Fictional commuting times of the same 20 employees.

The same times, 20-minute classes

These are exactly the same data, grouped into twenty-minute classes. Ten employees take under twenty minutes, nine between twenty and forty, only one over forty. The peak between ten and twenty minutes has vanished.

The choice of classes changes the shape

The choice of classes changes the shape. Too wide, and they hide the details. Too narrow, and they show bumps that are just chance. When you see a histogram, always check the width of the classes.

Unequal widths: area = count

One last trap: classes of different widths.

Misleading build: height = count, unequal classes

Now let's put everything over twenty minutes into a single class, from twenty to fifty. It holds ten employees. If we use the count as the height, this big bar dwarfs the rest: long commutes seem to dominate.

The right height: density

In a histogram, the area stands for the count. So the height must be a density: the count divided by the width of the class. For the class from twenty to fifty minutes: ten divided by thirty, about zero point three three employee per minute.

\[\text{density}=\dfrac{\text{count}}{\text{class width}}\]

Height = density: the picture is right again

With density, the picture is right again. The class from ten to twenty minutes is the densest, as in the first histogram. And the area of each bar gives back its count.

Area of each bar = count: 0.3 × 10 = 3; 0.7 × 10 = 7; ⅓ × 30 = 10 (height 0.33 rounded).

Exercise

40 visitors: aged 0–20: 8, 20–30: 12, 30–60: 20. Which class is densest?

Your turn. Forty visitors to a fictional museum: eight are aged zero to twenty, twelve are twenty to thirty, twenty are thirty to sixty. A histogram that uses the count as height highlights the thirty to sixty group. Is it right? Which class is the densest?

Solution

Divide each count by the width of its class. Zero to twenty: eight divided by twenty, zero point four visitor per year of age. Twenty to thirty: twelve divided by ten, one point two. Thirty to sixty: twenty divided by thirty, about zero point six seven. The densest class is twenty to thirty: the histogram based on counts was misleading.

  • Aged 0–20: 8 ÷ 20 = 0.4 visitor per year of age
  • Aged 20–30: 12 ÷ 10 = 1.2 visitors per year of age
  • Aged 30–60: 20 ÷ 30 ≈ 0.67 visitor per year of age
Answer : Ages 20–30: the densest class

Key points

Key points. For categories, a bar chart with separate bars. For numbers grouped into classes, a histogram with touching bars. And if the classes are unequal, the height must be a density: it is the area that counts.

  • Categories: separate bars
  • Numbers in classes: histogram, touching bars
  • Unequal classes: height = density
