Can you average postcodes? Types of variables

Does the average of two postcodes mean anything? And is 20 °C twice as hot as 10 °C? A number isn't always a quantity: sometimes it's a label, sometimes a rank. We learn to classify a variable and to know which operations make sense on each measurement scale. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled". Learning objectives

  • Classify a variable: nominal or ordinal categories, discrete or continuous quantities
  • Say which operations make sense on each scale: nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio

Prerequisites

  • S1.02: population, unit, variable

Can you take the average of postcodes?

Can you take the average of two postcodes? And is twenty degrees twice as hot as ten degrees?

CHALLENGE

(75001 + 13001) ÷ 2 = 44001. Does this result mean anything?

Let's do the sum with two French postcodes, one in Paris and one in Marseille. Seventy-five thousand and one, plus thirteen thousand and one, divided by two: forty-four thousand and one. Does this result mean anything?

A number isn't always a quantity

To answer, you need to know what type of variable you're dealing with.

Four types of variables

There are four main types of variables. A nominal variable is a label, with no order: a postcode, a colour, a nationality. An ordinal variable is a rank: a satisfaction score from one to five, an exam grade. A discrete variable is counted: the number of children in a family. A continuous variable is measured: a height, a duration, a weight.

  • Nominal: a label, with no order
  • Ordinal: a rank, with an order
  • Discrete: you count
  • Continuous: you measure

The first two are categorical, the other two numerical

Nominal and ordinal variables are called categorical: they describe a category, even when it's written with digits. Discrete and continuous variables are numerical: they measure a quantity.

Solution

The answer to the challenge. A postcode is written with digits, but it's a label: a nominal variable. The result, forty-four thousand and one, doesn't point to any place halfway between the two cities. Averaging labels makes no sense.

  • A postcode is a label: a nominal variable
  • 44,001 describes no "average" place
Answer : No: averaging labels makes no sense

Which operations make sense?

Each type of variable allows some operations and not others. These are called measurement scales.

Four scales, from least to most informative

There are four scales. On a nominal scale, you can only say whether two values are equal or different, and count them. On an ordinal scale, you can also rank them. On an interval scale, you can also measure differences: temperature in degrees Celsius. On a ratio scale, you can also say twice as much: kilograms, euros, durations.

  • Nominal: equal or different
  • Ordinal: also, rank
  • Interval: also, measure differences
  • Ratio: also, say "twice as much"

Is 20 °C twice as hot as 10 °C?

Let's compare. Twenty kilograms really is twice ten kilograms, because zero kilograms means zero mass. But zero degrees Celsius isn't the zero of temperature: it's the temperature at which water freezes, a reference point chosen by convention. On a scale whose zero truly means nothing, kelvins, going from ten to twenty degrees means going from two hundred and eighty-three to two hundred and ninety-three kelvins: about three and a half percent more, not double. That ratio doesn't describe how warm it feels: it only shows that twenty degrees isn't double ten.

  • 20 kg = 2 × 10 kg: zero means "nothing"
  • 0 °C is not a true zero of temperature
  • In kelvins: 293.15 ÷ 283.15 ≈ 1.035

0 °C = 273.15 K; zero kelvin is absolute zero.

A score from 1 to 5: averaging assumes equal gaps, say so

What about satisfaction scores from one to five? People often average them. But that assumes the gap between one and two is the same as the gap between four and five, which nothing guarantees. If you do it, say so, or give the share of each score instead.

Exercise

Hotel stars, from 1 to 5: which type? Is a 4-star hotel twice as good as a 2-star one?

Your turn. Hotel stars, from one to five: what type of variable is this? And is a four-star hotel twice as good as a two-star one?

Solution

Stars give a rank: it's an ordinal variable. You can say a four-star hotel is ranked above a two-star one. But saying it's twice as good makes no sense: stars don't measure a quantity.

  • Stars are a rank: an ordinal variable
  • You can rank, not compute a ratio
Answer : Ordinal: "twice as good" makes no sense

What to remember

Let's recap. A number can be a label, a rank or a quantity. The measurement scale tells you which operations make sense. And to say twice as much, you need a scale with a true zero.

  • A number can be a label, a rank or a quantity
  • The scale tells you which operations make sense
  • "Twice as much" needs a true zero
