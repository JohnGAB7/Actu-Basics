S4-03

Counting without listing: the multiplication rule

How many 4-digit codes exist, and how many ways are there to arrange five books? In many probability problems, counting comes first. With an imaginary tree of choices, we meet the multiplication rule, factorials and arrangements. And, above all, when order matters, and when it does not. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional data. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

Learning objectives

  • Apply the multiplication rule
  • Compute a factorial and an arrangement
  • Say whether order matters in a given situation

Prerequisites

  • S4.01: what is a probability

How many 4-digit codes? No need to list them all.

We want to know how many 4-digit codes there are. Far too many to list them one by one. How do we do it?

A tree of choices

Picture a tree. At each level, a choice.

4-digit codes

A four-digit code. The first digit can be zero to nine: ten possibilities. The second: ten, independent of the first. The third: ten. The fourth: ten. Each branch splits into ten new branches. Total: ten thousand possible codes.

  • Digit 1: 10 possibilities (0 to 9)
  • Digit 2: 10 possibilities
  • Digit 3: 10 possibilities
  • Digit 4: 10 possibilities
  • Total: 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000

The multiplication rule

Here is the rule.

total = n₁ × n₂ × … × nₖ

If you make k successive choices, and at the i-th step you have n_i possibilities, independent of the previous ones, then the number of possible outcomes is the product of the n_i. This is the multiplication rule.

N = n_1 × n_2 × ... × n_k

When choices shrink: the factorial

A new problem: how many ways to arrange five books on a shelf?

Arranging 5 books

Five choices for the first position. Once placed, four books remain for the second, then three, two, one. The product is one hundred and twenty. This product has a name: five factorial, written five exclamation mark.

  • Position 1: 5 books available
  • Position 2: 4 books left
  • Position 3: 3 books
  • Position 4: 2 books
  • Position 5: 1 book
  • Total: 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120

n! = n × (n−1) × … × 1

The factorial of n is the product of the integers from one to n. It counts the number of ways to arrange n objects in a row, taking order into account. Zero factorial is one by convention: there is one way to arrange nothing.

n! = n × (n-1) × (n-2) × ... × 1

Arranging k out of n: arrangements

What if we only want a few of them?

A podium from 10 runners

Ten runners, three podium steps. Ten choices for gold, nine for silver, eight for bronze. Product: seven hundred and twenty. This is an arrangement: three runners picked from ten, and order matters, because gold is not bronze.

  • Gold: 10 runners available
  • Silver: 9 runners left
  • Bronze: 8 runners
  • Total: 10 × 9 × 8 = 720

A(n, k) = n! / (n−k)!

The arrangement of k out of n, A(n, k), is n factorial divided by (n − k) factorial. It counts the ways to pick k objects out of n, in a given order.

A(n, k) = n! / (n-k)!

Does order matter?

Before multiplying, one question.

Two questions to ask

Are the objects all different? A die has six distinct faces, a podium three distinct medals: yes. Does order change the result? For a 4-digit code, yes: one two three four is not four three two one. For a podium, yes: gold is not bronze. If one answer is no, the multiplication rule must be adjusted: that is next lesson, combinations.

  • Are the objects interchangeable, or distinct?
  • Does the result depend on the order, or only on the list?

Classic mistake

A very common mistake.

Add or multiply?

Multiply when you chain independent choices one after another. Add when you split disjoint cases that cannot happen at once. Mixing the two gives absurd results: a 4-digit code does not have forty possibilities, it has ten thousand.

  • Multiply when choices come one after another
  • Add when there are disjoint cases
  • Mixing the two gives absurd results

Your turn

Your turn.

Exercise

How many different podiums with 8 runners?

Eight runners at the start. How many different podiums are possible, counting gold-silver-bronze order? Pause.

Solution

Eight for gold, seven for silver, six for bronze. Product: three hundred and thirty-six. This is the arrangement of three out of eight.

  • Gold: 8 runners available
  • Silver: 7 runners left
  • Bronze: 6 runners
  • Total: 8 × 7 × 6 = 336
Answer : 336 podiums

Key points

Key points. The multiplication rule: when chaining independent choices, multiply their counts. The factorial counts ways to arrange n objects in a row. The arrangement of k out of n counts ways to pick k with order. And always ask first: does order matter?

  • Multiplication rule: multiply the choices in each step
  • Factorial n!: n × (n−1) × … × 1
  • Arrangement A(n, k): choose k out of n with order
  • Before multiplying: does order matter?
