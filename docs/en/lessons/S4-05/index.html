Events: complement, union, intersection

At least one six in four tosses of a die: how likely? With an imaginary Venn diagram and an old problem posed by the Chevalier de Méré to Pascal and Fermat in 1654, we learn how to handle events. The complement turns "at least one" into "none". The union formula avoids double counting. Worked exercise at the end. Fictional data. Series "Reading numbers without being fooled".

Prerequisites

  • S4.01: what is a probability

At least one 6 in 4 dice tosses: what's the probability?

I toss a fair die four times. What is the probability of at least one six? At first, many say four out of six, or sixty-seven percent. The right method is quite different.

An event is a subset

First, vocabulary.

Within the sample space

For a fair die, the sample space is the six faces: one, two, three, four, five, six. An event is a subset: for example, 'get an even number' is {two, four, six}. Its probability is the share it covers: three faces out of six, that is, one half.

  • The sample space: all possible outcomes
  • An event: a subset of outcomes
  • Its probability: the share of the space it covers

The complement: go through the opposite

First rule, the one that solves the die problem.

P(A) + P(not A) = 1

The probability of an event plus the probability of its complement — everything that is not the event — always equals one. In other words, P(A) equals one minus P(not A).

\[P(A) + P(not A) = 1\]

Solve the die problem

Translating 'at least one six' directly is complicated: you would have to count cases with one, two, three, four sixes. But its opposite is simple: 'no six at all', that is, 'not a six' four times in a row. On each toss, five of six faces are not a six: five sixths. The four tosses are independent, so we multiply: five sixths to the fourth, about zero point four hundred and eighty-two. One minus that: zero point five hundred and eighteen, or fifty-one point eight percent.

  • Event A: at least one 6 in 4 tosses
  • Complement: no 6 in 4 tosses
  • P(no 6) = (5/6)⁴ ≈ 0.482
  • P(at least one 6) = 1 − 0.482 = 0.518
  • That is 51.8%

A bit of history: Pascal and Fermat

This problem is not recent.

The Chevalier de Méré, in 1654

In 1654, a gambler, the Chevalier de Méré, writes to Blaise Pascal. He wins by betting on at least one six in four tosses of a die. He loses — narrowly — by betting on at least one double six in twenty-four tosses of two dice. Why? Pascal and Fermat exchange letters. And their correspondence founds what is today called probability theory.

  • He bets: at least one 6 in 4 tosses
  • He loses: at least one double 6 in 24 tosses of two dice
  • He writes to Blaise Pascal
  • Pascal corresponds with Pierre de Fermat
  • Their letters found modern probability

Union: when events overlap

Second rule, for combining two events.

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)

The probability that one of the two events happens — or both — is the sum of their probabilities, minus the probability that they both happen. Why subtract? Because otherwise the intersection is counted twice, once in each term.

\[P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A ∩ B)\]

Red card or face card in a deck of 52

In a deck of fifty-two cards: twenty-six red, twelve face cards, six cards that are both red and faces. By the formula: one half plus three thirteenths minus three twenty-sixths. Thirty-two fifty-seconds, about zero point six hundred and fifteen.

  • P(red) = 26/52 = 1/2
  • P(face) = 12/52 = 3/13
  • P(red and face) = 6/52 = 3/26
  • P(red or face) = 1/2 + 3/13 − 3/26
  • = 32/52 ≈ 0.615

Disjoint: the formula simplifies

A key special case.

If A and B cannot happen together

If two events cannot happen together — we call them incompatible, or disjoint — their intersection has probability zero, and the formula reduces to the sum. For example, on a die, 'roll a one' and 'roll a six': just add one sixth to one sixth, that is, one third.

  • P(A and B) = 0
  • P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)
  • Example: rolling a 1 or rolling a 6 on a die

Classic mistake

A very common mistake.

Beware the forgotten intersection

People add P(A) and P(B) without subtracting the intersection. That double-counts the cases where both happen, and gives a result too large. Before adding, always check: are these events disjoint?

  • Adding P(A) + P(B) without subtracting P(A and B)
  • Gives a result too large
  • Check disjointness before adding

Exercise

Probability of at least one 1 in 3 tosses of a fair die?

At least one one, in three tosses of a fair die. Use the complement. Pause.

Solution

No one in three tosses: five sixths cubed, about zero point five hundred and seventy-nine. One minus that: zero point four hundred and twenty-one. In other words, forty-two percent. Far from three out of six.

  • Complement: no 1 in 3 tosses
  • P(no 1) = (5/6)³ ≈ 0.579
  • P(at least one 1) = 1 − 0.579 = 0.421
  • That is 42.1%
Answer : About 42.1%

Key points

Key points. For 'at least one', think complement 'none'. For a union, subtract the intersection, unless the events are disjoint.

  • P(A) + P(not A) = 1: go through the complement
  • P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B): subtract the intersection
  • If disjoint, the intersection is zero: just add
