Module S1

Statistics · reading numbers

Vocabulary, percentages, making sense of everyday numbers.

See the full programme →
Lessons in this module
S1-01

How many people live in Paris? What counts as data

How many people live in Paris: 2.

Open →S1-02

Who is this number about? Population, unit, variable

"People in France sleep 7 hours a night": who are we talking about, and what was measured? To understand a statistic, ask three questions: which population, which unit, which variable.

Open →S1-03

Can you average postcodes? Types of variables

Does the average of two postcodes mean anything? And is 20 °C twice as hot as 10 °C? A number isn't always a quantity: sometimes it's a label, sometimes a rank.

Open →S1-04

How many unemployed? Where numbers come from

How many people are unemployed in France? The answer depends on how they're counted: a household survey and a register of jobseekers don't measure the same thing.

Open →S1-05

2.4 million answers, and wrong: selection bias

In 1936, the American magazine Literary Digest mailed out about 10 million ballots to predict the presidential election.

Open →S1-06

Why do drug trials assign treatment at random?

Do coffee drinkers live longer, and if so, is it thanks to the coffee? A study that observes people who chose for themselves can't settle it.

Open →S1-07

Bike theft reports up 51%: did thefts really increase?

In a fictional town, reported bike thefts jump 51% in one year.

Open →S1-08

1% or 50%? Percentages and points

A rate goes from 2% to 3%: did it rise by 1% or by 50%? Both answers can be defended, as long as you say "percent of what".

Open →S1-09

Same number, disaster or detail? Reading a chart

The same price index can look like it is soaring or flat: it all depends on the axis.

Open →S1-10

Can “anonymous” data give you away?

Removing the name from a file isn't enough to make it anonymous.

Open →S1-11

“A one-in-ten chance”: what does it mean?

A one-in-ten chance doesn't mean “on the tenth try”.

Open →S1-12

A million measurements, and still wrong? Noise and bias

A scale that always adds two kilos is very consistent, and always wrong.
